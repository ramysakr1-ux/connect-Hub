Sounds vs Letters Lead-in8 min

Listen to each word and write it down. Then check your spelling with a partner and discuss what each word means. Finally, count the letters and the sounds in each word.

Example: letter = six letters (L-E-T-T-E-R) but four or five sounds, depending on British or American pronunciation.

ListenWordLettersSounds

Discuss in pairs: what does this activity show us about the sound–spelling relationship in English?

Answer

English spelling is not phonetic: the number of letters and the number of sounds rarely match. One sound can be spelt in several ways (ph = /f/), one letter can represent several sounds, and some letters are silent. This is exactly why learners need the phonemic chart — it shows sounds, not spelling.

Consonant Sounds — Where Is the Obstruction? Try it10 min

Consonant sounds are formed when the airflow from the lungs is obstructed by the moveable parts of the mouth — the tongue and the lips. Say each sound and decide where the obstruction happens. Example: /b/ as in ban is made by stopping the airflow at the lips.

See it happen. Seeing Speech (University of Glasgow) has MRI and ultrasound video of a phonetician producing every IPA sound. Press a symbol on their chart to watch the tongue and lips in action.

Voiced or Unvoiced? Practice8 min

Many consonants come in voiced / unvoiced pairs, such as /p/ and /b/. A voiced sound is made by activating the vocal cords — put your hand on your throat and feel them vibrate. An unvoiced sound needs no vocal cord activation.

Read each word in phonemic script, write it in normal spelling, then decide whether its first and last consonant sounds are voiced or unvoiced.
