A lesson taught through a story

This is a third conditional lesson for a B1 class, and nobody writes a rule on the board first. The class hears a story, is asked one question they cannot answer without the structure, and the sentence comes from them. Everything after that, the meaning, the form, the drilling and the three practice stages, stays inside the same story.

Go through the nine steps in order as the students would. Do each activity yourself; you will see what the technique asks of a class, and why it holds together, better than by reading about it.

Level
B1 — they have already met the first and second conditional
Main aim
To clarify and practise the third conditional in the context of a story
Time
60 minutes
Materials
The picture, and the sentence halves for step 6
The one rule for this lesson Don't explain the grammar before your students have produced it. The whole lesson depends on them saying the sentence first, even imperfectly. If you explain it in step 1, there is nothing left for them to do in steps 2 to 9.
A note on the story Somebody dies in it. Nothing is described in any detail, but read the beats in step 1 before class and decide whether it suits your group. If you'd rather soften it, say she was attacked and badly hurt — every sentence in the lesson still works.
Step 1 · 6 minutes

The story

Books closed, nothing on the board. The picture goes up and the story is told, not read. Listen to it with the map in front of you.

The story, beat by beat
  1. Two people, together since school. Quiet, private, very much in love. They marry and move abroad.
  2. They buy a house in the forest, outside the city. A mortgage. Two ways across the river: the bridge or the ferryman's boat.
  3. He finds work in the city. She can't find any. She stays at home.
  4. Interest rates rise. He takes extra hours, then more. He comes home late, and tired.
  5. She is alone all day. No job, no friends. She begins to think he has stopped loving her.
  6. Their anniversary. He promises to finish early — then calls: he's on nights, home tomorrow.
  7. An old friend is in town, phones to check on her, hears her crying, invites her to dinner.
  8. She walks across the bridge, buys a bottle of wine, and goes to his house.
  9. One bottle, then another. One thing leads to another. They sleep together.
  10. She wakes in the middle of the night, full of guilt, and leaves without waking him.
  11. She has no money for the boat. The ferryman won't take her — he promised his family: payment first, always.
  12. So she walks to the bridge.
  13. A stranger has escaped from the hospital and has not been found. He is on the bridge. She dies there.
  14. The police investigate. The friend knows. The husband knows. The ferryman knows. Now your class knows.
While you tell it Point at the picture as you go — it carries the geography so your words don't have to. Keep their eyes on you, not on a handout. Before you move on, check they have the map: How many ways are there across the river? Can she walk over the bridge? Does the ferryman take people for free?
Watch the clock This is the step that overruns. Six minutes of storytelling is generous. Ten minutes means you have done all the talking and your students have produced nothing.
Step 2 · 6 minutes

The question

One question, the one the story was built for. Try to answer it properly: you will find you need a structure to do it.

Ask: who is most responsible for her death?

The wife
She left the house
The husband
He worked through the night
The friend
He let her walk home
The ferryman
He refused to take her
The stranger
He was on the bridge

Then ask why, and push for a full sentence

If she had stayed at home, she would have survived.
One sentence on the board — take it from a student, then tidy it up together
What you'll get, and what to do with it Take two or three answers. Most classes produce something close on their own: "If she stay home, she don't die." That is what you work on — reshape it with them until the sentence is accurate, and keep it theirs. Write that one sentence on the board whole, and don't start analysing it yet.

If nobody gets near it, give them the first half — "If she had stayed at home…" — and let them finish it.
Step 3 · 8 minutes

Check the meaning

The sentence is on the board. Answer these questions about it; nobody explains it first.

If she had stayed at home, she would have survived.
1  Did she stay at home?
2  Did she leave the house?
3  Did she survive?
4  Are we talking about the past, now, or the future?
5  Can anyone change what happened?
6  "She stayed at home" — is that real or imaginary?
What they should end up with We are imagining a different past. The condition did not happen, so the result did not happen either — the sentence says the opposite of what really took place. It is finished and unreal: nobody can change it now. We use it to look back and express regret, blame or relief.
Never ask "do you understand?" It has only one answer and it tells you nothing. Every question above has a right answer you can actually hear.
Step 4 · 8 minutes

Clarify the form

Build the pattern from the sentence on the board. Click a word, then click a gap.

If + subject + + ,   subject + + +

Then show them they can turn it round

This is the point students miss most often, so give it a moment of its own. The if-clause does not have to come first. Start with the main clause instead, and the comma disappears.

If-clause first

If she had stayed at home, she would have survived.

Comma between the two clauses.

Main clause first

She would have survived if she had stayed at home.

No comma. Same meaning, same grammar.

How to get it across Write each half on a separate strip of paper and physically swap them over on the board, rubbing the comma out as you do it. They see that only one thing changes. Then ask them to say two or three of the practice sentences the other way round.
PointOn the board
ContractionsIf she'd stayed at home… ('d = had)  ·  …she'd have survived. ('d = would). Same sound, two different words — say so, because they will meet both.
NegativeIf she hadn't left the house…  ·  …she wouldn't have died.
QuestionWould she have left the house if she hadn't been so lonely? — the main clause inverts, and the if-clause goes second with no comma.
Other modalsshe might have survived / she could have taken the boat — same shape, less certain.
The classic errorIf she would have stayed… → If she had stayed… No would in the if-clause. Expect it and head it off.
Coming up in step 6One of the cut-up sentences is passive — she wouldn't have been killed (would + have + been + past participle). Deal with it if a student asks; don't front-load it here.
Teach less than this Six points are listed; three are teachable in one lesson. Take the pattern, the contraction and the word order, and leave the rest. Whatever you put on the board you will have to check later, so board only what the practice needs.
Step 5 · 4 minutes

Model it and drill it, backwards

The sentence is drilled from its last chunk back to its first, so the rhythm survives. Click to build it, and say each chunk as it appears.

The result clause

  1. survived /səˈvaɪvd/
  2. have survived /əv səˈvaɪvd/
  3. would have survived /ˈwʊd əv səˈvaɪvd/
  4. she'd have survived /ʃid əv səˈvaɪvd/

The if-clause

  1. home /həʊm/
  2. at home /ət ˈhəʊm/
  3. stayed at home /ˈsteɪd ət ˈhəʊm/
  4. if she'd stayed at home /ɪf ʃid ˈsteɪd ət ˈhəʊm/

What to drill for

  • have → /əv/, never /hæv/. At speed it reduces further: would have survived → /ˈwʊdə səˈvaɪvd/. If they learn the strong form here, they will never recognise the structure when someone says it.
  • had → 'd — she'd stayed. Most students can't hear it at all, which is exactly why drilling beats explaining.
  • Stress on the content words: if she'd STAYED at HOME, she'd have surVIVED.
  • Model → whole class → individuals → whole class again. Then go straight into step 6 while it's still in their mouths.
The trap It is very easy to get through this structure without ever saying it at natural speed. If your drill produces "if she HAD stayed at home, she WOULD HAVE survived" — every word strong — your students now own a sentence they can write and will never use. Drill the reduction, not the spelling.
Step 6 · 6 minutes · Controlled practice

Match the two halves

In pairs, match each beginning to its ending. Every sentence comes from the story, so it checks the grammar and the plot at once. On screen, click one from each column to join them.

Beginnings

Endings

While they work Let them say the sentences aloud as they match — it keeps step 5 alive. When you take feedback, ask who said it: sentences 2 and 3 are the husband, 5 is the friend, 4 and 6 are the ferryman, 7 is the stranger. That gives you a second pass over the meaning for nothing.

Number 8 is the only question, and it's the form they find hardest. Leave it until last and drill it again.
Step 7 · 7 minutes · Semi-controlled practice

Finish the sentences your own way

Same story, but now the ending is yours. Complete each beginning however you like, as long as the grammar holds. In pairs first, then a few aloud.

Possible answers — there are many more
  • If she had found a job in the city, she would have made some friends.
  • If he hadn't worked every evening, he would have noticed she was unhappy.
  • If they hadn't bought the house in the forest, they wouldn't have needed such a big mortgage.
  • If she had taken some money with her, the ferryman would have taken her across.
  • If the ferryman had broken his promise, she would have got home safely.
  • If her husband had come home that night, none of this would have happened.
How to run it Do the first one together on the board so they can see how much freedom they have. Then set a time limit — five minutes is plenty — and monitor. You are listening for two things: had + past participle in the first half, would have + past participle in the second. Correct those on the spot and save everything else for the end.

Ask for two or three different endings to the same sentence. It shows the class that the grammar is fixed and the idea isn't.
If they finish early Give them one about themselves: If I hadn't come to class today, … It takes the structure out of the story and into their own lives, and it warms them up for step 8.
Step 8 · 10 minutes · Freer practice

Rank them, and argue about it

Groups of three or four. Agree an order from 1 (most responsible) to 5 (least). One condition: you may only defend a position with a third conditional. Nothing to write; this stage is for talking.

How to run it Give them two minutes alone to decide their own order first, or the loudest voice in the group sets it and nobody else says anything. Then open the group discussion, then one spokesperson per group reports back.

Stay out of it. Monitor, write down what you hear and keep it — someone will say "If he would have come home…", and that goes on the board at the end, not in the middle of the conversation.

Groups almost never agree, and that is the point. The disagreement is what keeps them using the structure.

How to read it Whoever they blamed most is the value they hold least. Whoever they let off is what they value most highly. Blame the wife and love comes last; the husband, work; the ferryman, money; the friend, desire; the stranger, power.
Why it's worth five minutes It costs you almost nothing and it buys three things: everyone wants to speak again, the whole hour becomes memorable, and they leave having used the structure on something that mattered to them. Keep it light — it's a party trick, not a diagnosis, and it's worth saying so.

Finish here with your delayed correction. They will still be talking, so you will have their attention for it.
