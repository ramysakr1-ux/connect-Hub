Aims

An aim isn't good because it starts with the right words. It's good because someone reading it knows exactly what you're teaching and in what context. Decide on each one.

Procedure

You are not writing notes to yourself. You're writing so that someone else could pick up your plan and teach the stage without you in the room, which is why it's bullet points, imperatives, and clear at every line who is doing what.

Running it

StageWhat you do
AimsThe four criteria on screen. Each aim: Ready to teach from, or Needs work; the feedback says why. Do the first two together, the rest in pairs.
ProcedureSame again. The one to stop on: a procedure a colleague could not teach from.
Interaction patternsMatch each description to the pattern. Ind, PW, GW, T-S, S-S, OC, OCFB.
Anticipated problemsLesson plan or language analysis sheet. The rule is on the page: management, tasks, materials, technology and timing on the plan; meaning, form and pronunciation on the sheet.
RecapRead the four boxes aloud; they are the checklist for their next plan.

The key

Every item carries its own feedback on the page. Start again resets a part.

Watch for

  • Aims that start with the right words and say nothing. "By the end of the lesson students will be better able to…" followed by nothing specific is the one they all write.
  • Procedures in the future tense ("students will discuss"). Imperatives, bullet points, timed.

Sources. The aims, procedures and problems to judge are the tutor's own, written for this course's lesson plan and language analysis sheets.
