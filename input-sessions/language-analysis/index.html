Language analysis: filling in the sheets

The two sheets, box by box: the target language, meaning and how you check it, form, pronunciation, the problems, and the vocabulary grid. Then a sheet to fix and the last check.

Step 1

You have two sheets, not three

Everyone counts three. There are two.

Language Analysis — Grammar and Functional Language

One sheet for both. A structure (present perfect for experience) and a function (making suggestions) get analyzed the same way: target language → meaning → form → pronunciation → problems.

Language Analysis — Vocabulary

A grid instead of boxes, because you analyze each item separately. Use it when vocabulary is the main aim or a significant one — including a sub-aim in a skills lesson.

So which one would you use?

Read each aim and choose the sheet.

The one that catches people out A reading lesson with a short pre-teach of four items still needs the vocabulary sheet. "Significant" doesn't mean "main" — if you are going to clarify it, you analyze it.
Step 2

The target language box

Three rows, and they are not the same thing. Most of the problems on this sheet start here.

RowWhat it wantsWhat goes wrong
Language pointThe structure or function, narrowed to the use you are teaching, and which forms you'll cover."Present perfect." Which use? Affirmative only, or questions too?
Model sentencesThe actual sentences you will put on the board, in context, with real words.Writing the rule instead: "We use the present perfect for experiences." That is not a sentence a student would say.
ContextThe situation you build, plus the eliciting question that pulls the sentence out of them."I will talk about travel." That's a topic, not a context, and there's no question in it.

Which of these is a model sentence?

Language point
Present perfect simple for life experience, with an unspecified time. Affirmative, negative, and question with ever.
Model sentences
I've been to Italy.
I haven't been to Japan.
Have you ever been to Egypt?
Context
A world map on screen with three pins in it — Italy, Egypt, Peru. I tell the class these are places in my life, and that Japan has no pin: not yet.

Eliciting question: "Italy — in my life, yes. When? Last year? Ten years ago? It's not important. So how do I say it? I ______ to Italy."
The test for a context Could a colleague pick up your sheet and run the first two minutes of your lesson from it? If not, it isn't specific enough yet.
Step 3

Meaning: concept, then checking

The box asks for two different things, and people answer the second one twice.

  • Concept — what this language means, here, in words a student could follow. Not the grammar label, not the form.
  • Checking techniques — your CCQs with the answers written in, plus any timeline, cline or picture you'll use. Draw them.

Which is the concept for "I've been to Italy."?

Are these good concept checking questions?

Same sentence: I've been to Italy.

What makes a CCQ work Short. Yes/no, either/or, or a one-word answer. Simpler language than the thing you're checking. It never contains the target language — if the student has to understand the structure to answer the question, it checks nothing. And on the sheet, every question has its answer next to it in brackets.
Also on this sheet A timeline for most tenses, a cline for anything gradable (furious → angry → annoyed), a picture or a quick sketch for concrete lexis. "I will draw a timeline" is not a timeline. Draw it on the sheet — pen on paper and a photo is fine.
Step 4

Form: show the breakdown

The box says show how you will break down the form in the lesson. That means what goes on the board, not the name of the tense.

Build the form line

Click the parts in order for I've been to Italy.

Affirmative
Subject + have/has + past participle  →  I have been to Italy.
Contracted: I've been / She's been — this is the form they will hear.
Negative
Subject + haven't/hasn't + past participle  →  I haven't been to Japan.
Question
Have/Has + subject + ever + past participle?  →  Have you ever been to Egypt?
Short answers: Yes, I have. / No, I haven't.
Worth flagging
been ≠ gone. Been = went and came back. Irregular participle, so it has to be learned, not worked out.
Functional language is the same job, different parts For making suggestions you break down the fixed pattern, not a tense:
Why don't we + bare infinitive? → Why don't we go out?
How about + verb-ing? → How about going out?
Shall we + bare infinitive? → Shall we go out?
Mark what is fixed and what the student can change. That is the whole point of a functional analysis — and it's where How about to go comes from.
Step 5

Pronunciation: the feature, and how you'll show it

Two halves, and the second one is usually missing. "I will drill the sentence" answers neither.

Which feature is each note about?

Model sentence
I've been to Italy.  /aɪv ˈbɪn tə ˈɪtəli/
Features
Contraction: I have → I've /aɪv/ — one syllable, and the form they'll actually hear.
Weak form: to → /tə/, never /tuː/.
Sentence stress: I've BEEN to Italy — stress on the participle and the place.
Question: Have you EVer been to Egypt? — rising intonation, have weak /həv/.
How I'll highlight it
Model the sentence at natural speed first, then back-chain from Italy. Mark the stressed syllables with circles on the board and show the contraction with finger highlighting (two fingers → one). Choral, then individual, then choral again.
The giveaway If your pronunciation box could be copied onto any other lesson's sheet without changing a word, you haven't analyzed your language — you've described drilling.
Step 6

Anticipated problems — but which ones, and where?

There are two problem boxes in your pack and they take opposite things.

On the lesson plan page

Problems with tasks, materials, technology, classroom management, interaction. The sheet says it outright: NOT language.

On the language analysis sheet

Problems with the meaning, form and pronunciation of your target language — and nothing else.

Where does each one go?

And the solution has to be something you do

ProblemSolution
Students may confuse been and gone.✗ I will explain the difference.
✓ Contrast two board sentences — He's been to the shop (he's here) / He's gone to the shop (he's not here) — and check with "Where is he now?"
Students may say I have been to Italy in 2019.✗ I will correct them.
✓ Pre-empt it in the CCQs — "Do we say the exact time? (No)" — then put the wrong sentence on the board at the error-correction stage and let them repair it.
Students may use the strong form /hæv/ throughout.✗ I will drill it.
✓ Model the contracted form only, back-chain from the end, and use finger highlighting to show I have → I've.
The pattern A solution names a technique and a moment in the lesson. "I will explain / correct / drill" names neither.
Step 7

The vocabulary sheet

Same thinking, different shape: one row per item, six columns, and every column gets something.

ColumnWhat it wants
Vocab itemThe word, stress marked, phonemic transcription, part of speech. All three — this is where most marks are lost.
ConceptThe meaning at your learners' level. Not the dictionary entry copied out.
ContextHow you'll convey it: the picture, the situation, the example sentence.
Clarification of meaningCCQs with answers, visuals, examples — how you check they've got it.
FormPart of speech in use: countable or not, gradable or not, collocations, dependent prepositions, the verb pattern that follows.
PhonologySyllables, stressed syllable, any sound likely to be difficult, any silent letter.

One row, done properly

Vocab item
exhausted  (adj)   ex·HAUS·ted   /ɪɡˈzɔːstɪd/
Concept
Extremely tired — so tired you can't do anything else.
Context
Photo of a runner lying on the ground at the end of a marathon. "She ran 42 kilometres. Now look at her."
Clarification
Is she a little tired or very tired? (Very)
Can she run another race now? (No)
Is this a strong word or a weak word? (Strong)
Form
Adjective. Extreme adjective, so it isn't gradable — absolutely / completely exhausted, not very exhausted.
Phonology
3 syllables, stress on the second. /ɡz/ in the middle, not /ks/. Weak /ɪ/ in the last syllable. The h is silent.

Vocab item
commute  (verb)   com·MUTE   /kəˈmjuːt/
Concept
To travel to work and back every day, usually a long way.
Context
Map showing a house in a village and an office in the city, with an arrow back and forth and "every day" written under it.
Clarification
Do I do this every day or once a year? (Every day)
Am I going to work or on holiday? (To work)
Is it a short walk or a long journey? (Long)
Form
Regular verb. Commute to work, commute from the suburbs. Also a noun — a long commute. Person: a commuter.
Phonology
2 syllables, stress on the second. Schwa in the first — /kə/, not /kɒ/. /mjuː/ with the y sound, like music.
Don't analyze what you aren't teaching Six or eight items is a lesson. Fifteen is a list. Analyze the ones you are actually going to clarify, and say so in your aims.

Model sentences: We use the present perfect to talk about experiences.
Context: I will talk about traveling and ask students about their holidays.
Concept: have / has + past participle
CCQs: Do you understand? Is it clear?

Reference materials

The box is not decoration, and "the internet" is not a reference. Name the book, the edition and the pages you used, in your own words on the sheet.

  • Swan — Practical English Usage. For "is this right, and why?"
  • Parrott — Grammar for English Language Teachers. Written for us: includes the problems learners have.
  • Scrivener — Teaching English Grammar. Meaning, form and pronunciation with teaching ideas attached.
  • A learner's dictionary — Cambridge or Oxford online. Your source for transcription, stress and level-appropriate definitions.

The last check

One habit worth having Write the language analysis before you write the procedure. If you can't say clearly what the language means, how it's formed and how it sounds, you can't plan the stages that clarify it — and it shows in the lesson, not just on the page.
