Eliciting and CCQs

Getting language out of students instead of putting it in: why we elicit, a game on eleven ways to do it, a dialogue elicited on film, how a CCQ is built, a worksheet, other ways to check meaning, then your turn.

Spot the elicitor Lead-in

Two teachers, same target word. Read both, then click to reveal who is eliciting and who is telling.

why bother?
Why do we elicit? Discuss, then reveal

Teacher B took longer than Teacher A. So why bother? In pairs, list as many reasons as you can for eliciting instead of telling. Then reveal and compare.

  • Students already know something. Most have met English before, so they are not empty vessels. Eliciting switches on what they already know.
  • It shows respect. Asking says: you have something to bring to this lesson.
  • It keeps students involved. They are working on the language, not just listening to it.
  • It cuts teacher talking time. Long explanations often go over students’ heads.
  • It shows what they really know. What students can or cannot produce tells the teacher where to go next.
But remember: you can’t elicit what isn’t there. If two or three tries get nothing, give the word yourself and move on.

In the classroom: a dialogue the students build Watch, then name it

A teacher gets a whole dialogue out of a class without reading them a line of it. Watch once, with the eleven techniques in front of you. Which do you see, and what do the students say that the teacher never said?

Write as you watch. The techniques you spotted, in order, and the moment you would have given up and told them.

Afterwards, in pairs
Compare your lists. Where did the teacher drill before moving on? What did the board look like at the end, and who wrote it? Keep one thing from this film for your next TP, and say what it is.
Building CCQs Model, then two tasks

A concept checking question checks that students have understood the meaning. Here is how one is built.

The sentenceHe managed to open the jar.
It meansHe found it difficult to open the jar, but he did open it.
In simpler languageHe opened the jar. It wasn’t easy.
Turn them into questionsDid he open the jar? (Yes)  Was it easy? (No)
Task A · What is the sentence?

Each set of CCQs checks one sentence. Write the sentence, then reveal.

Task B · What is wrong with these CCQs?

Discuss what is wrong with each set, and write a better one. Then reveal.

The key

Every task’s answer is on the page behind its reveal. The practice words in Your turn have no key: any technique that gets the word out of a student is right, and any CCQ with a short answer that does not contain the word is right.

Watch for

  • CCQs that contain the target language (“Did he use to bite his nails?”). Send them back to meaning.
  • “Do you understand?” dressed up as a CCQ. A CCQ has an answer you can hear.
  • CCQs about things the sentence doesn’t say: Task B’s first set is the model of that mistake.
  • Eliciting that becomes a guessing game with no context. The scenario does the work.
