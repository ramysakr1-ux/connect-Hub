To begin

Drama in the ELT classroom is…

“(Dramatic activities) … give the students the opportunity to use his or her own personality in creating the material on which part of the language class is based. These activities draw on the natural ability of every person to imitate, mimic and express himself or herself through gesture. They draw, too, on the student's imagination and memory, and natural capacity to bring parts of his or her experience that might never otherwise emerge. They are dramatic because they arouse interest, which they do by drawing on the unpredictable power generated when one person is brought together with others.”
“Drama attempts to put back some of this emotional content into language – and to put the body back too. This does not mean that we must suddenly start leaping about the room in an exaggerated fashion, but it does imply that we need to take more account of meaning.”
“Drama techniques have the singular merit of directly engaging students' feelings and, as a result, often make them aware of the need to express them more appropriately.”
“Drama can help us to restore (this) totality by reversing the learning process, that is, by beginning with meaning and moving to language from there….”
“The problem of not wanting to speak or, more often, not knowing what to say is practically resolved…”
“It forces us to take as our starting point life not language.”
“From the evidence to hand there is little doubt that these techniques are an extremely powerful motivational factor.”
“Drama may involve music, history, painting, mathematics, skiing, photography, cooking – anything. It does not respect subject barriers.”
“Drama helps us to keep thirty people active all the time…”
“It helps students to gain self-confidence….”
“The student-teacher relationship improves …”
“These activities help to build up trust and foster group information.”
“The language generated is realistic…”
“Drama, then, can help considerably by ensuring that language is used in an appropriate context, no matter how ‘fantastic' this context may seem ….”

Warm-up

Non-verbal icebreakers and warm-up activities

Shake hands as if…

  • You're meeting for the first time at a formal meeting
  • You think the other person is trying to cheat you
  • You want to show how important you are
  • You are best friends that haven't seen each other for a long time
  • You are very rich men/women at a very expensive cocktail party
  • You think the other person smells terrible
  • You're dying to go to the toilet!
  • You're meeting your new boss and you're trying to impress
  • You think the other person is the most gorgeous person you have ever seen!
  • You're meeting your fiancé's parents for the first time
  • You're meeting a rapper

Variations

  • Make the instructions more sedate: “Shake hands as if you're about to listen to something important,” “Shake hands as if you're about to enter an exam.”
  • Make it verbal and turn it into a name-learning activity for a new class: “My name is …….”

Mirroring

  • In pairs, stand opposite each other.
  • Both students raise their hands to shoulder height with their palms facing outwards.
  • Make sure their palms are close, but not touching.
  • One student starts to move his/her hands with his/her palms facing outwards.
  • The other student must follow the ‘leader' as accurately as possible.
  • Ensure that both students take it in turns to be the ‘leader.'

The See-Saw

  • Students work in threes.
  • One stands behind the other.
  • The one in front falls backwards, with their eyes closed, and the one behind catches him/her and returns them to an upright position.
  • After a few falls, partners should change places.
  • When students feel comfortable, change the pairs into threes, with the middle person being rocked between the two.

Eels in the Grotto

  • Briefly explain what a grotto is.
  • Organise the students into groups of 8 or 10.
  • One group organises itself to form a ‘grotto', a shape with holes in it.
  • The others are ‘eels' or ‘fish' who crawl through the ‘holes.'

Blind

Type Non-verbal or verbal
  • Distribute ‘obstacles' around the room: chairs, tables, books, bags, and so on.
  • Pair up your students.
  • One student is blindfolded or has his/her eyes closed.
  • The ‘sighted' partner takes the ‘blind' partner around the room by holding his/her arm. No talking is allowed if you are doing this as a non-verbal activity.
  • Partners change over and repeat the process.

As a verbal activity

  • Useful for practising instructions, adverbs and there is / there are: “There are a lot of books here, so walk carefully, lift your feet, turn left” and so on.
Getting started

A couple of simple activities to build up your confidence

Miming a Monster

Aims parts of the body, cooperation, physical coordination Age most ages Level beginner or elementary
  • Revise parts of the body if necessary.
  • Ask two students to come to the front of the room.
  • Explain that they are going to make a monster according to your instructions.
  • Describe the monster and the volunteers make a monster using parts of their bodies.
  • Repeat the activity with the whole class.
  • Students work in pairs.

Possible follow-up

  • Get students to draw pictures of the monsters and make a display.
  • Students work in groups and make a monster. One student gives the instructions, the others follow.
  • The teacher looks around the class, describes a monster, and the others guess which one.
Adapted from Sarah Phillips, Drama with Children (Oxford Resource Books for Teachers)

Listen and Mime

Aims listening to a story for specific words or phrases, following directions Age 6–12 Level beginner upwards

Preparation

  • Find a suitable story.
  • Practise telling it to someone else.
  • Choose key words from the story and think of suitable actions to go with them.
  • Practise telling the story with a colleague to make sure the actions are practical: not too long or difficult, boring or dangerous.

Procedure

  • Tell the students you are going to tell them a story, but they need to learn some actions first.
  • Get the children to stand in a circle, or behind their desks if the room is too small, and join them.
  • Teach them the words and actions, a few at a time, and revise them before you tell the story.
  • Tell the story and get the students to join in with the actions.
  • Tell the story again in another lesson if you think it is necessary.

Possible follow-up

  • Make a cartoon strip or a book of the story.
  • Give a written version of the story with gaps.
  • Make pictures and ask the students to put them in order.
  • Ask students to think of a different ending.
  • Make the story into a mini-play, with props: masks, hats, old clothes, puppets, etc.
Adapted from Sarah Phillips, Drama with Children (Oxford Resource Books for Teachers)

Rod the Frog

Pictures frog (green), butterfly, parrot, witch, rainbow

Tell the story using pictures. The basic version is like this:

This is the story of Rod the frog.

Rod the frog is a beautiful green frog, but he is very sad.

‘Green is horrible.'

‘Hello, Rod. Why are you sad?' asks the butterfly.

‘Green is horrible. I want to be like you,' says Rod the frog.

‘Go and see the witch at the end of the rainbow. She can change your colour.'

‘That's a good idea. Goodbye.'

‘Goodbye.'

‘Hello, Rod. Why are you sad?' asks the parrot.

‘Green is horrible. I want to be like you,' says Rod the frog.

‘Go and see the witch at the end of the rainbow. She can change your colour.'

‘That's a good idea. Goodbye.'

‘Goodbye.'

Rod the frog goes to the end of the rainbow.

‘Hello, Rod. Why are you sad?' asks the witch.

‘Green is horrible. I want to be like the butterfly or the parrot,' says Rod the frog.

‘Ha, ha, ha! No problem. I can change your colour. Are you ready?'

‘One, two, three. Abracadabra!' Whoosh, bang!

‘Now I'm like the butterfly… Oh, no … it's horrible!'

‘Let's try again. One, two, three. Abracadabra!' Whoosh, bang!

‘Now I'm like the parrot… Oh, no … it's horrible! Oh, please, I want to be a frog! I want to be green! I want to be ME!'

‘OK, Rod. But this is the last time. One, two, three. Abracadabra!' Whoosh, bang!

‘Are you happy now?' asks the witch.

‘Oh, yes. I'm happy now. I'm a beautiful green frog! Thank you. Goodbye.'

by Carol Read and Ana Soberon

Cooling down

Non-verbal cooling-down exercises

Feeling my Space

  • Students lie, ideally, or sit, with their eyes closed.
  • The teacher tells them to feel the space they occupy, in a firm, calm voice.
  • Tell the students to try to expand their bodies to fill the space as much as their arms and legs will allow.
  • Finally, tell them to reduce themselves in order to occupy as little space as possible.

From Seed to Plant

  • Ask students to stand up and find their own space.
  • Tell them to curl themselves up to be as small as possible.
  • Now ask the students to unravel themselves, very, very slowly, into a plant stretching up towards the sun.
  • You can also use classical music: Sibelius or Brahms.

Don't Break it!

  • Students stand together in circles of about 10.
  • Ask them to imagine that there is a very large, fragile plate in the middle of their circle.
  • Ask them to pick it up together and carry it very carefully to another part of the room.
To finish

The Birthday Party

  • Students go out of the room.
  • They come in one by one, greeting the host (the teacher), pretending they are guests at a birthday party.
  • Some will pretend to be bringing presents.
  • Some will pretend to be old friends, others will be new and need to be introduced, etc.
  • Encourage everyone to mingle and chat about the party, each other, and so on.
  • To add a bit of spice, ask everyone to imagine they are having this party as if they are in Spain, the UK, the USA and so on.

Interplanetary Greetings

  • Pair up students; each pair has to represent its own planet.
  • Each pair decides on:
    • a name for their planet
    • a way of exchanging greetings on that planet
  • Each pair demonstrates their greetings to the others.
  • The teacher then sets up a party, with the students changing partners and greeting each other in the manner of their planets.
Trainer notes Tutor only

Before

Space: chairs to the walls. The Activity Evaluation Sheet open on a device per trainee; they fill it in after each set. Music for From Seed to Plant if you have it. Say at the start that nobody has to do anything they do not want to; the See-saw and Blind need trust, so watch them.

The key

None. The sources are on the page: Maley and Duff for the quotations, Phillips for the two children’s activities, Read and Soberón for Rod the frog.

Watch for

  • The See-saw with the wrong partner. Same height, and you spot the first few falls.
  • Laughter turning to self-consciousness. Keep the pace up; the cool-down is where it settles.

Sources. The opening quotations are from Alan Maley and Alan Duff, Drama Techniques in Language Learning (Cambridge University Press). "Miming a monster" and "Listen and mime" are adapted from Sarah Phillips, Drama with Children (Oxford University Press); "Rod the frog" is by Carol Read and Ana Soberón.
