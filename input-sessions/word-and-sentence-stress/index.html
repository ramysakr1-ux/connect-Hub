Lead-in: one line, nine readings Watch twice

Nine actors, one line from Hamlet, and a disagreement about where the stress goes. First time through, tick the word each one lands on. Second time, in pairs: what does the line mean each time, and which reading would you choose?

From Shakespeare Live! From the RSC (BBC Two, 23 April 2016). If the frame stays black, watch it on YouTube; the RSC's own short cut has the ending.

What the sketch does

Each actor is handed a different word and a different reading: a contrast, a doubt, a question of identity. The Prince of Wales ends it by landing on question, the one word nobody else had used. One line, nine meanings, and the stress did all the work. That is the whole of this session: in English, where the weight falls is part of what you mean.
rhythm
The rhythm of English Say it aloud

Say each line below out loud. However many extra words you pack in, try to keep the beat exactly the same length, like a drumbeat with more or fewer notes squeezed between the same four hits.

COOK   BAKE   FRY   ROAST

COOK and BAKE and FRY and ROAST

COOK and then BAKE and then FRY and then ROAST

COOK and then it's BAKE and then it's FRY and then it's ROAST

Click to reveal: why does the rhythm stay the same?
English is a stress-timed language — the stressed words fall at roughly equal intervals of time, no matter how many extra words are squeezed in between. The stressed words here (cook, bake, fry, roast) are content words. The words that get squashed to keep the rhythm even (and, then, it's) are grammar words. This is one reason natural English speech sounds so different from reading each word separately.
Word stress: primary and secondary Click each word

Say each word to yourself first. Where do you think the strongest stress falls? Some longer words also have a lighter, secondary stress somewhere else. Click each word to check.

primary stress secondary stress unstressed

First, sort them. Before you click anything, say each word and put it under its pattern. Big bubble for the main stress, small for the rest. Then click the words to check.

Check the sort
Oo London, Turkey · oO Brazil, Japan, Torquay · Ooo Hungary, Italy, Germany, India, Switzerland · oOo Chicago · oOoo Australia, America, Slovakia · small, then O Pakistan, Montreal (a secondary stress on the first syllable).

Australia and America may not fit. Some speakers say aus-TRAY-lya in three syllables, and America can lose a syllable in fast speech. The main stress does not move; the count does. Accept both.
How to mark stress on the board
Use bubbles or boxes above the word, a big one on the stressed syllable. Not an accent mark and not an underline: Ss read an accent as a letter in their own language, and underlines are already doing other jobs on your board. Mark it the same way every lesson so Ss know it on sight.
Contrastive stress: one sentence, seven meanings Say it seven ways

Say the sentence below seven times, stressing a different word each time. Before you check, ask yourself: what does stressing that word seem to imply?

Stress in context: say it, then see it in a conversation Two steps

Step 1. Say each question on its own and mark the word that carries the main stress.

What's your name?

Nice to meet you.

How are you?

Where are you from?

Where do you live?

Step 2. Fill in the gaps with your own details. Read the conversation aloud with a partner, then mark the stress again. Has it moved?

A Hi, I'm . What's your name?

B I'm .

A Nice to meet you.

B Nice to meet you too.

A How are you?

B Fine, thanks. How are you?

A Good. Where are you from?

B . And where are you from?

A . Where do you live now?

B In . And where do you live?

Reveal: what moved, and why
On their own: what's your NAME, nice to MEET you, how ARE you, where are you FROM, where do you LIVE.

Asked back, the stress jumps to YOU: nice to meet YOU too, how are YOU, and where are YOU from, where do YOU live. The question is old news by then; the person is the new information.

So a phrase taught on its own can teach the wrong stress. Give Ss the conversation, not the list.
The café: one answer, five times Mark the main stress

The server says exactly the same words every time. Read each exchange, decide where the main stress falls in the answer, then check. Better still, read it aloud with a partner and argue.

Customer A flat white with oat milk, please.

Server We don't have any oat milk.

Where is the stress?
On OAT MILK. It is the news: everything else is fine, this one thing is missing.

Customer Oh, never mind. Just a splash, then.

Server We don't have any oat milk.

Where is the stress?
On ANY. Not even a splash: the answer corrects how much.

Customer Is it in the fridge? Let me have a look.

Server We don't have any oat milk.

Where is the stress?
On HAVE. It isn't hidden somewhere; it isn't in the building.

Customer You must have some. You always have some!

Server We don't have any oat milk.

Where is the stress?
On DON'T. A flat denial of what the customer insists on.

Customer Every café in town has oat milk!

Server We don't have any oat milk.

Where is the stress?
On WE. Maybe every other café does; this one doesn't.
What the café shows
The words never change; the conversation decides where the weight goes. Each time, the stress lands on the part of the answer that pushes back against what was just said.

Noun or verb? Choose the stress Quiz

Some words are spelled the same whether they're a noun or a verb, but the stress moves. For each sentence, pick which syllable you think is stressed.

Make it productive Plan it

Look back at the tasks above. Your Ss would only hear stress in most of them: listen, mark, check. That is receptive work. Pick one task and write how you would get Ss to say it in a lesson.

Some ways in
  • Drill it: clap or tap the beat, back-chain a long phrase from the end, then say it at natural speed.
  • The café in pairs, then again with a new item and a new customer of their own.
  • Ss write a sentence, stress one word, and their partner says what it implies.
  • Board the bubbles first, have Ss say the words before you model them, then correct.
  • Noun or verb: Ss write one sentence with each and read them to the group, who say which they heard.
When you plan pronunciation for TP, plan the moment Ss say it, not only the moment they notice it.
Round-up Three things

Before you open it, write the three things this session showed about stress.

Check
  1. English is stress-timed. Stressed words keep the beat; grammar words get squashed to fit.
  2. Sentence stress moves with the context and with the speaker's attitude. Teach it in a conversation, not a list.
  3. Word stress has patterns, and they can change with the part of speech: a REcord, to reCORD.

The key

On the page for every task. Primary stress: aus-TRA-lia, HUN-ga-ry, bra-ZIL, ja-PAN, IT-a-ly, LON-don, pak-i-STAN (secondary on pak), GER-ma-ny, chi-CA-go, mon-tre-AL (secondary on mon), tor-QUAY, TUR-key, IN-di-a, SWIT-zer-land, a-MER-i-ca, slo-VA-ki-a.

Watch for

  • Stress shown by volume alone. Length and pitch carry it too; the chant is where they feel that.
  • Trainees who mark every word. One stress per thought group; contrastive stress moves it.
  • Australia and America in the sort. Some accents give them a syllable less; the main stress stays put. Accept both.
