What the sketch does

Each actor is handed a different word and a different reading: a contrast, a doubt, a question of identity. The Prince of Wales ends it by landing on question, the one word nobody else had used. One line, nine meanings, and the stress did all the work. That is the whole of this session: in English, where the weight falls is part of what you mean.

The rhythm of English Say it aloud

Say each line below out loud. However many extra words you pack in, try to keep the beat exactly the same length, like a drumbeat with more or fewer notes squeezed between the same four hits.

YOU   ME   HIM   HER

YOU and ME and HIM and HER

YOU and then ME and then HIM and then HER

YOU and then it's ME and then it's HIM and then it's HER

Click to reveal: why does the rhythm stay the same?
English is a stress-timed language — the stressed words fall at roughly equal intervals of time, no matter how many extra words are squeezed in between. The stressed words here (you, me, him, her) are content words. The words that get squashed to keep the rhythm even (and, then, it's) are grammar words. This is one reason natural English speech sounds so different from reading each word separately.

The key

On the page for every task. Primary stress: aus-TRA-lia, HUN-ga-ry, bra-ZIL, ja-PAN, IT-a-ly, LON-don, pak-i-STAN (secondary on pak), GER-ma-ny, chi-CA-go, mon-tre-AL (secondary on mon), tor-QUAY, TUR-key, IN-di-a, SWIT-zer-land, a-MER-i-ca, slo-VA-ki-a.

Watch for

  • Stress shown by volume alone. Length and pitch carry it too; the chant is where they feel that.
  • Trainees who mark every word. One stress per thought group; contrastive stress moves it.
