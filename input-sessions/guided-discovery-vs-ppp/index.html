lesson one
Read for the gist One minute

Read the text quickly. Is Frank unlucky, or lucky? Decide in one sentence, then compare with a partner.

The unluckiest traveller in the world

Frank, a music teacher from a small town, says he is the unluckiest traveller alive. In 1962 he was on a train when it came off the rails and fell into a river. A year later, as he was travelling on an airplane, the door came open and he fell out. He landed in a haystack. In 1966 the bus he was on skidded off the road, into another river. Then his car caught fire while he was driving along the motorway; he got out seconds before the petrol tank exploded. Two more cars, a lorry and a bus later, he stopped travelling. In 2003 he bought his first lottery ticket. He won a million.

Watch a lesson Before you watch

A teacher teaches a grammar point to a class of adults. As you watch, work out the stages of the lesson: how many there are, where each one starts, and what happens in it. Keep brief notes in the boxes below. Add a stage when you see one begin.

which was which
Two approaches Discuss, then write

You have now sat in two lessons. The first taught one use of the past continuous; the second taught a different point. The language is beside the point: almost any grammar or vocabulary could have been taught either way. With your partner, talk through these.

  1. Which lesson do you think used a guided discovery approach?
  2. Which lesson used a PPP (present, practise, produce) approach?
  3. Which approach did you prefer? Why?
  4. In which approach was it easier to understand the target language? Why?
  5. Which approach was used inside a text-based lesson? Why does it have to be used inside a text-based lesson?

Which was which

Lesson one was guided discovery. The language came out of a text you had already read for meaning; you worked out the rule from two marker sentences and completed the form yourself.

Lesson two was PPP. The teacher built a situation, elicited and presented the language, had the class practise it under control, and then had them produce it.

the stages
The stages of each Fill the gaps

Each text describes the stages of one approach. Fill the gaps with words from the box. One word is not needed.

Guided discovery

Stage 1. Introduce the target language in . The language will often be introduced in the form of a listening or a reading. The language will be contextualised and students should still be able to answer the questions despite possibly being with the target language. This stage is set up like any reading or listening task.

Stage 2. Using a sentence taken from the reading or listening, the students answer a series of questions that will help them to discover the meaning and use of the target structure. Because the marker sentence is taken from context, the students should be able to work out why it is being used and what it is used to talk about. This is why context is paramount. using sentences that are not connected to the original context.

Stage 3. The students complete a series of exercises to practise the target language. Often the context for the controlled or freer speaking exercise will or be related to the original context.

PPP

Stage 1. The teacher language from the students with cues, to see what they already know. Often some of the students know a lot of, even all of, the . If no one knows any of the words for this topic, the teacher provides some, but usually a good portion of the target phrases can be obtained from the students. Why do this? Eliciting and cueing the students makes the topic, and your class, more relevant to them. After all, they you the material they are going to practise and learn. When things feel relevant, students are more motivated. The teacher then puts that lesson's target language up on the , perhaps as grammar, in charts, or written in dialogues. The presentation stage features more than the other stages of the lesson. Even so, less teacher talk, in any part of the lesson, is better. The goal is to have the students talk, not the teacher.

Stage 2. Next comes the Practice stage. The students practise the target language in one to three activities. These progress from very structured, with little room for error, to less structured as the students master the material. The activities should include as much as possible and not focus on written work, although written activities can sometimes give a structure for the spoken practice. Student talk should take most of the time; teacher talk as little as possible.

Stage 3. The third stage, Production, is when students the target language and use it in conversations they ideally create and structure themselves. They can now talk about themselves, their daily lives or situations using the language they have just learned. Student talk takes as much as of the time.

The key

On the page: the ten discovery answers, the rule (two, the same time, past continuous, past simple), the form table, and the two gap texts (guided discovery: context, unfamiliar, normal, marker, Avoid, controlled and freer, mirror; PPP: elicits, target vocabulary, gave, board, teacher talk, practice, further, 90%). The stage notes on the film have no key on the page; this is the lesson, for the room.

The filmed lesson (present simple, third person, an ordinary day). 0:00 to about 1:00, the situation. A magazine picture: this is Kamal; where does he work? A stall, not a supermarket. "I'm going to tell you about an ordinary day for Kamal. Every day." 1:00 to 3:10, presentation and drilling, one sentence at a time. A clock drawn on the board; "He get up in five o'clock" from a student; the teacher takes the idea, asks for a better sentence, models "He gets up at five o'clock", choral and individual repetition, corrects "gates" and "five o'clock", then "Tell Daniel", "Tell Bruna". A cup drawn: "he takes a coffee" becomes "He drinks a cup of coffee", told to Francesca. A bicycle drawn: "How does he go to work?" "Your bike" becomes "He cycles to work", with "cycle" taken as the right verb in the wrong form, and "to walk" corrected to "to work". About 3:10 to the end, the first move towards production. "Go back to the beginning. Can you remember the first sentence about Kamal?" Kamal is written on the board; the class rebuilds the four sentences in order and tells the person next to them. So: a situation built from one picture and three drawings, each sentence elicited, corrected, modelled and drilled, then the whole set retold in pairs. The clip ends before anything freer; let them notice that production never fully arrives.

Watch for

  • Trainees who name the approaches early. Let it pass; the point is the comparison, not the guessing.
  • The film: trainees who count every activity as a stage. Push them to the three big moves and what changes at each.
  • "Presentation" heard as a lecture. The teacher on film elicits from a picture and drawings; point at that.
