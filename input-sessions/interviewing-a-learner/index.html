Interviewing a learner

You will interview a student for your Focus on the Learner assignment. This session is about what to ask, how to ask it so they can answer, and what to write down, so that what you bring back is evidence you can use.

what you need
What the assignment needs from you Match, then check

Cambridge’s syllabus describes the Focus on the learner assignment in its own words. In brief, it asks you to look at one learner, or one group, and to show:

  • how their background, previous learning and learning preferences affect their learning;
  • what their language and skills needs are;
  • where they could get development and personal support outside the class;
  • and activities you would suggest for them, with a reason for each.

The interview is where much of that comes from. Below are the eight questions you will ask (more on them in section 4). For each, choose the area it mostly helps you with.

    asking clearly
    Asking so they can answer Rewrite, then compare

    Each question on the left would work for a teacher and fails for a student at A2. Rewrite it in your own simple words, then reveal one way to do it.

    Four habits that help

    • Short words, one question at a time, then wait. Count to five before you help.
    • Ask open questions (what, when, how, why). If you need a yes or no, follow it with “Can you tell me more?”.
    • If they do not understand, say it again in simpler words. Do not translate, do not correct.
    • When they say something useful, stay on it: “Can you say that again?”, “For example?”.

        try it
        Try it, then write it up Pairs

        In pairs, one of you is a student at about A2, the other the interviewer. Ask three of the eight questions, one at a time, and follow up at least once. The student answers as a real student would, with the sort of mistakes you have just sorted. Then swap.

        On the interviewer’s sheet, write down the student’s exact words for anything you might want to use. Then answer below, on your own.

        Trainer notes Tutor only

        Before

        Print the eight questions as a half-page sheet for each trainee (they are on the page, section 4, and in the session you run on Connect). Have a second sheet with space under each question for the student’s exact words. Decide beforehand which students are interviewed and by whom; the page does not do that for you.

        Running it

        StageWhat you do
        A first interviewRead Lina’s interview aloud, two voices. Individually, they rate the six interviewer lines, then Check. Take line 6 first: a leading question feels natural and costs the most.
        What the assignment needsRead the four bullets. Then the eight questions: they match each to an area, and Check. Say that the match is the main area; most questions serve two.
        Asking clearlyPairs. Each rewrites the six on paper before anyone reveals. Take two of their rewrites onto the board and compare with the models. Then the four habits.
        The eight questionsOpen them one by one. For each, one trainee says the follow-up in their own words, not the one on the card.
        What you heardSort Lina’s five, then the specific-or-vague five. The fifth sort item is the one to argue about: a listening need is evidence too, and it is not an error.
        Try itPairs, then swap. Interviewers write exact words. Collect one specific problem and its exact words from each on the way out.

        The key

        On the page: the six ratings (too complex for the level; fine; corrects instead of listening; leading and closed; two questions at once; does not follow up), the eight areas (background, background, previous learning, motivation, preferences, needs and support, needs and support, needs and support), the six model rewrites, the five sorts (grammar, grammar, a skills need not an error, pronunciation, grammar), and the five specific-or-vague (too vague, specific, too vague, specific, too vague).

        Watch for

        • Trainees who correct in the practice interviews. Stop it at once and ask what they gained by it.
        • Notes that summarise: “she has problems with the past”. Ask for the sentence she actually said.
        • A student who answers every question with one word. The follow-up is the skill, not the question.
        • The assignment is not the interview. The syllabus asks for the learner’s background, previous learning and preferences, their language and skills needs, support outside the class, and activities with reasons; the interview supplies the first three, and where the needs come from.
