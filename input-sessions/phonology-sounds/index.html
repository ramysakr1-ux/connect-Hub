Sounds against letters Lead-in8 min

Listen to each word and write it down. Then check your spelling with a partner and discuss what each word means. Finally, count the letters and the sounds in each word.

Example: letter = six letters (L-E-T-T-E-R) but four or five sounds, depending on British or American pronunciation.

ListenWordLettersSounds

Discuss in pairs: what does this activity show us about the sound–spelling relationship in English?

Answer

English spelling is not phonetic: the number of letters and the number of sounds rarely match. One sound can be spelt in several ways (ph = /f/), one letter can represent several sounds, and some letters are silent. This is exactly why learners need the phonemic chart — it shows sounds, not spelling.

Consonant sounds: where is the obstruction? Try it10 min

Consonant sounds are formed when the airflow from the lungs is obstructed by the moveable parts of the mouth — the tongue and the lips. Say each sound and decide where the obstruction happens. Example: /b/ as in ban is made by stopping the airflow at the lips.

See it happen. Seeing Speech (University of Glasgow) has MRI and ultrasound video of a phonetician producing every IPA sound. Press a symbol on their chart to watch the tongue and lips in action.

voicing
Voiced or unvoiced? Try it8 min

Many consonants come in voiced / unvoiced pairs, such as /p/ and /b/. A voiced sound is made by activating the vocal cords — put your hand on your throat and feel them vibrate. An unvoiced sound needs no vocal cord activation.

Read each word in phonemic script, write it in normal spelling, then decide whether its first and last consonant sounds are voiced or unvoiced.

Trainer notes Tutor only

Before

Test the sound on the room’s machine: open the page, use the voice picker at the top and press Try it. Pick a British voice that the room can hear clearly; the choice is remembered on that machine. The Seeing Speech link in part 3 needs the internet.

Running it

MinStageWhat you do
8Sounds and lettersPlay each word twice; they write it, then count. Let the room disagree about "letter" (four or five sounds) before you reveal: the disagreement is the lesson.
7TermsPairs match the six. Then say each definition in your own words with your hand on your throat for voiced.
10ConsonantsEveryone says the eight sounds aloud, hand to mouth, before choosing. Then Seeing Speech on the screen for two of them.
8VoicingPairs: read the phonemic script aloud first, write the word, then first and last sound. Throat check for every answer.
7The chartClick round the vowels in rows; ask them to feel the jaw drop from /iː/ to /ɑː/. Then the three places the consonant rows go.
10ScriptIndividually on their own devices, using the symbol keys. Check as a class; reveal only after.
10Film titlesPairs, first to finish. Then the question that matters: which of these would you use with a class, and at what level?

The key

All on the page: Check answers under each task, Reveal for the script and the films. The obstruction answers are: b, m and p at the lips; v at the lower lip and top teeth; n and t at the tongue tip and ridge; r at the tongue tip near the ridge; k at the back of the tongue and soft palate.

Watch for

  • The voice. A synthetic voice is fine for single words; it is not a model for connected speech, so say the words yourself too.
  • Trainees who stop at "I can’t do phonemic script". The keys on the page mean nobody has to draw a symbol; they only have to choose one.
  • American and British counts for "letter": both are right; the chart is British.

Sources. The terminology and the voiced-unvoiced task follow the tutor's CELTA input materials; the phonemic chart is the standard British English chart of 44 phonemes (Adrian Underhill, Sound Foundations, Macmillan).
