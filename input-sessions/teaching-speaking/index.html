do it first
Do it first: the end-of-course party A speaking task, as learners

Be the students for fifteen minutes. Your tutor runs it; follow the steps.

  1. One minute, in pairs. What was the best party you have ever been to? What made it good?
  2. Two minutes, on your own. Your class has €200 for an end-of-course party. Note one idea each for: where, food, and one thing to do. Notes only, no sentences.
  3. Language that might help. How about…? · I’d rather… · That’s a good idea, but… · Why don’t we…? · OK, let’s go for…
  4. Five minutes, in groups of three. Agree on one party for the whole class. Everyone has to agree, and you must stay within €200.
  5. Each group tells the room its plan. The room votes. Then your tutor puts a few sentences heard during the task on the board, and you put them right together.

Keep this task in mind. Parts 5 and 6 come back to it.

What makes a fluency task work? Diagnose, then rebuild

A trainee planned this speaking task for a B1 class. It flopped: two pairs finished in a minute, one student hardly spoke, and at the end there was nothing to say about it. In pairs, find everything that went wrong. Then rebuild the task so it works.

The teacher: “OK, now talk to your partner about holidays. You’ve got ten minutes.”

She sits down at her desk and marks homework while they talk.

Ten minutes later: “Finished? Good. Open your books at page 34.”

Nine things that make a fluency task work

  • A real reason to talk. Nothing had to be found out, decided or finished, so there was no reason to keep going. Give it a task to complete.
  • A gap worth closing. Nobody needed what their partner knew. An information or opinion gap makes them curious: “Find two things you’d both hate on a holiday.”
  • A clear context, set up first. “Holidays” is a topic, not a situation. Set the scene before they start.
  • Clear roles. Who asks, who answers, who decides? Nobody knew.
  • Free choice of language. Fine here, and keep it: in a fluency task they use anything they know, not one target structure.
  • Everyone speaks. Open pairs let one student lead. Roles, or a short task each, give the quiet one a turn.
  • The teacher monitors. Marking homework meant nothing was heard: no ideas to pick up, no language to note.
  • Feedback on the task. “Finished? Good.” says nothing. Ask about what they found out or decided.
  • A correction slot at the end. A few sentences heard during the task, on the board, put right together.

Compare with what you said in the warm-up. Which of these do you already build into your lessons? Which do you sometimes forget?

    the framework
    The productive skills shape Name it, then reveal

    The party task, and the twelve stages you just ordered, fall into five bigger stages. In pairs, before you look: name the five, and say what each one is for. The same shape works for writing.

    The five stages

    Main aim: to practice oral (or written) fluency in the context of ___. Stages are chosen as needed; not every lesson needs all five.

    Lead-inActivates what students already know about the topic and raises interest. (The best party you’ve been to.)
    Preparing to speak/writeGenerates ideas: time to brainstorm, make notes and think about what to say. May include reading or listening to a model of the task. (Your notes on the €200 party.)
    Useful languageLanguage they may find useful. This is not target language. It helps; they don’t have to use it. (How about…? I’d rather…)
    Speaking/writing taskDevelops fluency through the task itself. (Agree on one party within budget.)
    Feedback / error correctionContent and outcome first, then generic errors. (The vote, then the sentences on the board.)

    Add a pre-teaching stage before the shape if students lack essential vocabulary.

    production
    Plan a speaking lesson Apply the framework

    In pairs, choose a topic and plan the five stages using the productive skills shape. Keep it brief; a sentence or two per stage is enough.

    Share your stage plan with another pair. Would their lead-in genuinely prepare students for their task?

    One way to plan it

    Topic: a weekend away (B1).

    Lead-in: three photos of short trips (a city, a beach, a cabin in the woods). Pairs: which would you choose, and why? Two minutes, then a few answers.

    Preparing to speak: a minute to think about their last trip away: where, how they got there, the best and the worst moment. Notes, not full sentences.

    Useful language: “The best part was…”, “It turned out to be…”, “If I went again, I’d…”, on the board and said once or twice; not taught as new grammar.

    Speaking task: groups of three. Each tells the story of their trip; the others ask two follow-up questions each. Then the group picks the trip they would most like to go on and tells the class why.

    Feedback / error correction: content first (which trip won, and why), then four or five sentences heard during the task on the board, some good and some to fix, corrected in groups.

    Check yours the same way: the lead-in opens the task’s topic, and the useful language is what the task needs.

    wrap-up
    Key takeaways Yours first

    Write down the three things from today you will use in your next speaking lesson. Then compare with ours.

    Ours

    • Free (fluency) speaking and controlled (accuracy) speaking need different staging and different teacher roles.
    • A good fluency task is purposeful, has a clear context, and gives every student a chance to speak.
    • “Useful language” helps students; it is never target language they must use.
    • Feedback on a speaking task covers content first, then generic errors.
    • Not every stage of the productive skills shape is needed every time; choose as necessary.

    The key

    On the page: the sort (controlled: accuracy, non-communicative, one item, form, teacher intervention; free: the other five), the stage order (select the activity, set up the room, pre-teach, stimulus, instructions, check, groups, roles, preparation, activity, monitor, feedback), and the problems (confidence: atmosphere and small groups; vocabulary: preparation and task language; pronunciation: coping strategies; thinking quickly: preparation; dull topic: choose topics carefully and personalize).

    Coping strategies, examples for the room: paraphrase the word you can’t say ("the thing you open a bottle with"); swap it for one you can; spell it; show it with a gesture; slow down and chunk it; check it landed ("did you get that?"); ask ("how do you say…?"); hold the floor ("well, let me think…") so the pause is not a full stop. The message gets across when the pronunciation doesn’t, and the learner stays in the turn.

    Watch for

    • "Useful language" taught as the aim of the lesson. It is a crutch, offered and then ignored if they do not need it.
    • Feedback that starts with errors. Content first: did the task get done, what did they decide. Then language.
