Tense and aspect

Tense says when. Aspect says how the speaker sees it: finished, in progress, or looked back on from later. English has two tenses and two aspects, and coursebooks call every combination a tense. Work out the system, then use it on the structure you teach next.

What you just foundMost of these are about now, and almost none of them use a present form to say so. Time is in the world; tense is in the verb. The two often agree, and a learner who assumes they always do will go wrong in both directions.

The perfect aspect

Meaning. Each event is looked at from a later point: it happened before that point and matters at it. Have worked is seen from now; had left from a later moment in the past; will have finished from a point in the future.

Form. have (in some tense) + the past participle: ’ve worked, ’s broken, ’d left, will have finished.

Pronunciation. The auxiliary is nearly always contracted and weak: I’ve /aɪv/, she’s /ʃiːz/, they’d /ðeɪd/, will have → /wɪləv/. Learners hear none of it, and so say none of it.

The continuous aspect

Meaning. Each action is seen as in progress at some point, not complete, and usually temporary: in progress now, in progress when she came in, in progress at the time you ring, in progress up to now.

Form. be (in some tense) + verb-ing: ’m watching, are drawing, were throwing, ’ll be having, ’ve been waiting.

Pronunciation. Contracted and weak auxiliaries again: I’m, we’ll be → /wiːlbi/, we’ve been → /wɪvbɪn/. And the -ing: learners who say /ɪŋg/ or /ɪn/.

The future forms

Four ways of talking about the same holiday, none of them a tense. I think we’ll spend is an opinion, a prediction. We’re going to spend is an intention, decided already. We’re spending is an arrangement, fixed, probably booked. We’ll be spending presents it as the normal course of things, in progress at that time, nothing to discuss.

Form. will + base verb; be going to + base verb; present continuous; will be + verb-ing.

Pronunciation. we’ll /wiːl/ against we; going to → /gənə/ in speech; the weak to.

Say the name properlyPresent perfect is present tense plus perfect aspect. Past perfect continuous is past tense plus both aspects. Saying it that way, every time, is what stops a learner treating thirteen names as thirteen unrelated things.
What aspect adds Pairs from real use

Six pairs of sentences from a corpus of real English. In each pair, name the two underlined forms and say what the aspect adds to the meaning of the second one. Then reveal.

Swasey, who has two young children, works from home two days a week.
DH is working from home today and helped get the kids ready.
Early the next morning I drove to the cemetery.
On Sept. 25, he was driving to work when agents stopped him.
“Music is my life, and I love it.”
I’m reading a new novel now, and I’m loving it!
As a child, he learned to knit sweaters for himself.
So far in his life, he has learned Bahasa Indonesia, Irish Gaelic, Mandarin Chinese …
Bergman made films about the apocalyptic nature of war.
The 55-year-old Vendetti has made films and videos for 15 years.
Someone wrote “Californians Go Home” in the dust on their moving truck.
On the facade, someone has written “Proud to be Black”.

What each aspect adds

1. Present simple, present continuous. The continuous makes it temporary: today, not the usual arrangement.

2. Past simple, past continuous. The continuous puts the driving in progress, as the background the stop happened against.

3. Present simple, present continuous. Love is a state and is not usually continuous; making it continuous makes the feeling temporary and in progress, something happening now, with some force.

4. Past simple, present perfect. The perfect keeps the time open: his life so far, and the list could grow. As a child closed it.

5. Past simple, present perfect. Bergman’s career is over; Vendetti’s is still running, and for 15 years measures up to now.

6. Past simple, present perfect. The perfect brings the result into the present: the words are on the wall now. The dust has gone.

The continuous adds in progress, temporary, not complete. The perfect adds seen from a later point, with a connection to it. That is the whole system; the thirteen names are combinations of it.

The passive Not an aspect, but it goes with them

The same event, said two ways.

A man with a bicycle pump attacked the politician.

The politician was attacked by a man with a bicycle pump.

Say this one the second way, keeping the tense and aspect: The mechanics are servicing my car at the moment.

Present continuous, passive

My car is being serviced at the moment. The form is be + past participle, and be carries the tense and the aspect: is being, present continuous. The mechanics can go; everyone knows who services cars.

A learner’s sentences Correct, then explain

Six sentences from a learner’s piece about her life. Each has one problem with the verb. Write the correction and, in a few words, the reason. Then reveal.

  1. I live in Lima since 2019.
  2. When I was a child I was wanting to be a pilot.
  3. I have finished school in 2015.
  4. Last year I have been moving to a new flat.
  5. I am knowing my neighbors very well now.
  6. By the time I arrived home, the match already started.

The corrections

1. I have lived in Lima since 2019. From a point in the past up to now: perfect, not present simple. Since is the clue.

2. I wanted to be a pilot. Want is a state; states are not usually continuous.

3. I finished school in 2015. A finished time, in 2015, closes the door on the present perfect.

4. Last year I moved to a new flat. A finished time again, and a single complete event: no perfect, no continuous.

5. I know my neighbors very well now. Know is a state. Now tempts learners into the continuous; the meaning does not need it.

6. The match had already started. Earlier than another past point: past perfect. By the time and already both point to it.

Four of the six are about aspect, not tense. The learner knows when things happened; what she has not got is how English looks at them.

I’ve been painting the kitchen

The name. Present tense, perfect aspect and continuous aspect together: present perfect continuous.

The meaning. A continuous activity that began in the past and ran up to very recently, maybe not finished, with a result you can see now (sorry I look such a mess).

The timeline. A line with now marked. A wavy stretch from a point in the past up to now, or almost up to it, with a splash of paint at now for the result.

The CCQs. Did the painting start before now? Yes. Is it finished? We don’t know; maybe not. Can you see the paint on me now? Yes.

The form. subject + have/has + been + verb-ing.

The pronunciation. I’ve been → /aɪvbɪn/: the contraction and the weak been disappear together. Learners say I been painting or I have been in full.
