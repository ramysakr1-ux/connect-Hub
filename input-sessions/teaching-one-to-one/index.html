Teaching one-to-one

What changes when there is one student and no class: what still works, what one-to-one does better and worse, tuning in to a new student, the first lesson, your roles, correction with no audience, the student as the material, and what to do when it goes wrong. Then you plan a first lesson.

Will it transfer? Lead-in

You have taught these stages to a group on this course. Tomorrow the same lesson is for one student. Which stages work as they are, which need changing, and which won't work at all?

The patternAnything that needs a second student, or a teacher standing back to listen, has to change. You are the student's only partner, so you are in the conversation, not watching it.
Better one-to-one, or in a group?

For each point, decide where it is easier.

Why most of it comes down to adjustingTalking to one person, you can watch their reaction and change what you do at once: the topic, the pace, the level, your tone. With twenty people you can only do that for a few minutes each. One-to-one, adjusting is the whole job.
Tuning in to a new student

Your new student starts on Monday. This is everything the school has told you.

Paulo, 46. Operations manager at a shipping company. B1. Two lessons a week, 90 minutes each, in a meeting room at his office, for ten weeks. Since the company merged with an American firm he runs a weekly video call with the Chicago team. He has done "three courses with grammar books, and I still can't say what I mean in the meeting." He says he wants to talk, not do exercises.

What else do you need to know?

In pairs, write the questions you would ask him in the first lesson. Group them under five headings: who, where, when, what, how.

The questionWhat it coversFor Paulo, for example
WhoThe person: motivation, experience, expectations, personality, how they have learned before.What went wrong with the grammar books? What does he expect from a teacher? What is he good at?
WhereThe place, and what it allows: his office, your school, a café, out doing something. Where you sit.Will the phone ring? Can you sit at the corner of the table, not across it? Can he show you the screen he uses for the calls?
WhenTime of day, length of each lesson, how long the course runs. Fatigue in long lessons.After a full day, 90 minutes is long. What day is the Chicago call?
WhatGoals and needs: what he has to do in English, with whom, and what they will ask of him.Who is on the call? What does he have to report, ask for, agree to? Can he record one?
HowThe ways of working he wants, and the ones you can offer. He has a say.He wants to talk. Grammar can come inside the talking, from his own mistakes.

Need, lack or want?

Three different things. A need is what he has to do in English. A lack is where he falls short of it. A want is what he would like the lessons to be.

When the want and the lack disagreePaulo wants to talk; he lacks accuracy in the past tenses. Don't force a grammar lesson on him because it is good for him. Sell it, using his own call as the reason, or teach it inside the talking. If he isn't convinced, drop it and come back another way.
The first lesson

Put the first lesson with Paulo in order. Click the stages in the order you would do them.

    Plan three times more than you needOne-to-one, you can drop an activity the moment it isn't working, so take more than you will use: three times as many ideas is not too many. Note after each lesson what you actually did, and start the next lesson from it.
    Your roles

    With one student you are more than the teacher. Which role are you playing at each moment?

    The one that is hard to playIn a group, you set up a role play and listen. One-to-one, you are the other person in it: you have to keep the conversation real and notice the language at the same time. When you play a part, push back a little (be the customer who won't pay, the manager who says no) so the student has to use the language.
    Correction with no audience

    There is no class to lose face in front of, so you can be direct. There is also nobody else to share your attention, so every mistake you notice is a mistake you could correct. That is the trap.

    A ski instructor who taught groups for years took his nephew skiing, one to one. After every turn he called out something to fix: bend your knees, weight forward. By the third run the boy was in tears, and he spent the rest of the day skiing happily with a friend who taught him nothing at all.

    What went wrong, and what would you do in a lesson?

    He became the judgeBeing watched and corrected all the time is not learning, it is being evaluated. With his groups he gave a little instruction, then let them ski. In a lesson: let the student talk, and write what you notice on a sheet beside you. Go through it at the end, with the good things on it as well as the mistakes. The student takes the sheet home.

    What would you do?

    Reformulate it

    Correcting the words is not always enough: sometimes the whole sentence has to be said another way. Write what an expert speaker would say. Then reveal.

    1. "We are not a company of the cheap price. We are a company of the good quality."
    2. "The meeting was with much discussion but finally no decision was taken by anybody."
    3. "In my job the most part of my time is going for the answering of emails."
    One way to say each1. We don't compete on price; we compete on quality. 2. We talked a lot, but nobody decided anything. 3. I spend most of my day answering emails. Each one keeps the student's meaning and changes the whole sentence. Correcting word by word would have left something stiff and still not quite English.
    The student is the material

    A coursebook is written for thousands of students; your student is one. Their job, their documents, their week and their opinions can be the lesson. You can walk in with a pen and some paper.

    A lesson from nothing

    Paulo explains one part of his work to you: how a delayed shipment is handled. Put the lesson in order.

    Bring the words back

    From Paulo's last two lessons, these are in his notebook: a backlog, to chase (a supplier), a bottleneck, to fall behind, in hindsight. With no preparation, write three quick questions or tasks that bring them back.

    Some that need no preparationWhat else can fall behind? (Payments, a schedule, a student.) What causes a bottleneck in your warehouse? Who did you have to chase this week, and how? Is there a backlog on your desk now? What would you do differently, in hindsight, about last month's delay? None of them is a test: each one makes him use the word about his own work.

    The lesson record

    At the end of each lesson, the useful language goes on one page the student keeps: the new words in his own sentences, with the stress marked. Over ten weeks it becomes his own coursebook.

    If you use a coursebook

    One-to-one coursebooks are made to be dipped into. Read the contents page with the student and tick the units he needs; the contents page is the needs analysis.

    Your turn: a first lesson

    Choose one student. Plan their first sixty minutes, then explain your plan to a partner who chose a different one.

    Ines, 34

    A nurse, B2, moving to Toronto in four months to work in a hospital. Lessons online, twice a week. Worried about understanding patients on the phone.

    Kaito, 16

    A1 to A2, preparing for a school exchange in Ireland. His parents booked the lessons; he did not. Lessons at his home after school.

    Mrs. Haddad, 61

    A retired architect, B1. Wants to read English novels and talk about them. Lessons in a café near her home, once a week, no end date.

    Kaito, for exampleAsk about him, not about English: what he plays, watches, follows, and what worries him about Ireland. The first task is his: he shows you his phone's photos or a game and explains it, and you help with the words he reaches for. Correct almost nothing aloud; note it, and choose two things for the last five minutes. He takes away a short list of his own words and one thing to do before next time, chosen with him. The motivation he lacks is the first thing the lesson has to solve: his parents booked it, so the lesson has to become his.
