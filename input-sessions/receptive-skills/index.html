Staging a receptive skills lesson

A receptive skills lesson never starts cold. It opens by creating interest in the topic: visuals, personalisation, brainstorming what the class already knows. Background knowledge is part of how anyone understands a text, not a shortcut around it. Only then does the lesson pre-teach vocabulary, and only the words essential to understanding.

The gist task comes next, testing global understanding only. For reading, students read silently under a time limit, so they read quickly and concentrate on the gist rather than the detail; for listening, the recording plays once. Only once gist is secure does the lesson set a specific information or detail task, prompting closer reading or listening.

Peer checking before whole-class feedback plays a major role in a receptive skills lesson. It increases involvement, builds confidence by making correction feel safer than the teacher stepping in first, deepens understanding as students explain answers to each other, and builds the teamwork and communication skills that matter beyond the classroom.

The lesson closes with a follow-up task based on something related to the text: a language focus, a discussion, a role play or a written task.

Before the text

  • Discuss what students already know about the topic
  • Look at pictures and predict what the text will be about
  • Brainstorm ideas that might come up in the text
  • Pre-teach the words the tasks depend on, and only those

Gist

  • Match headings, key words or pictures to paragraphs, or to sections of the recording
  • Choose, or write, the best title for the text
  • Put the paragraphs, or the pictures, in order
  • Tick the topics that are mentioned

Detail and specific information

  • True or false, or short-answer questions
  • Multiple choice
  • Fill in a table or a chart with information from the text
  • A more demanding pass if the level and the text allow it: an inference task, reading between the lines

After the text

  • Discuss and personalize the topic: opinions, experiences, the same situation in their lives
  • A role play or a questionnaire built on the text
  • A game or a writing task that grows out of the text
Reading and listening: what changesThe staging is the same for both. What changes is the second look. A reader can look back at the text, so the detail task is set and then they read again at their own pace. A listener cannot, so set the detail task before the recording plays the second time, and let them hear it once more. For gist, each is one pass only: three minutes for a text, one play for a recording.

Write the tasks

One short text. In pairs, write a gist task and a detail task for it. Then swap with another pair and do theirs, without looking at the text for longer than the task allows. Is their gist task really gist? Is their detail task really detail?

The last bridge keeper · B1

Every morning at six, Anton Weiss unlocks the gate of the Ruda bridge, the last hand-turned bridge on the river. It has opened for boats for 112 years, and Anton has done the job for thirty-one of them. Boats must call him the day before: nobody will turn the bridge for fewer than two boats. In winter, when the river freezes, he spends his days painting the iron rails, one by one, so that the town never has to pay for a new bridge. “People ask me whether a machine could do it,” he says. “Of course it could. But then who would say good morning to the boats?”

When you swap: do the other pair’s gist task first, and stop at the time limit. Could you answer it without reading every word? Then do the detail task. Could you answer any of it without the text?

What to check, and one example of each

A gist task is answered from the whole text and ignores the details: “What is special about Anton’s job, and does he enjoy it?” One minute. If you need the numbers to answer it, it is a detail task.

A detail task sends learners back to specific places: How many years has the bridge been opening? (112.) How long has Anton done the job? (31.) Why must boats call the day before? (Nobody will turn it for fewer than two.) What does he do in winter? (Paints the rails.)

The faults to look for

A gist task that is really a detail question (“How many boats…?”). A gist task with no time limit, so learners read every word. A detail question the learners could guess without reading. Questions that follow the text in order, so the answers can be copied one after another. A task that tests vocabulary instead of reading.
