Cued dialogue: a phone call Less-guided practice

Work in pairs and follow the cues in order to build a natural phone conversation. Student A starts. Use each cue to write your own line.

Student A
Say who you are. / Ask how B is.
Student B
Answer the phone. / Reply.
Student A
Ask what B is doing at the weekend. Invite him/her to a concert.
Student B
You can't on Saturday. Say what you're going to do.
Student A
Suggest Sunday afternoon.
Student B
You can't. Say why. Suggest some time next month.
Student A
You can't. You're going abroad for work. Suggest a Friday evening.
Student B
Agree. Arrange a time and place to meet.
Student A
Say goodbye.

One way it could go

A: Hi, it’s Lina. How are you?
B: Oh, hi Lina! I’m good, thanks. And you?
A: Fine, thanks. What are you doing at the weekend? There’s a jazz concert on Saturday. Would you like to come?
B: I’d love to, but I can’t on Saturday. I’m going to help my brother move house.
A: Oh, OK. What about Sunday afternoon?
B: Sorry, I’ve got a family lunch on Sunday. How about some time next month?
A: I can’t next month, I’m afraid. I’m going abroad for work. What about a Friday evening?
B: Friday’s great. Shall we meet at the café by the station at seven?
A: Perfect. See you then. Bye!

Any lines that do the same jobs are right: inviting, refusing with a reason, suggesting another time, arranging, and going to for plans.

Types of drill Example, then your turn

Let's look at one together first: a chain drill.

Chain drill: practice passes from student to student like a chain. One asks or says something to their neighbor, who responds and then turns to the next student.
T → S1: How are you?
S1 → S2: I'm fine, thanks. How are you?
S2 → S3: I'm fine too. How are you? ...and so on around the room.

going further
More meaningful drills Read, then make it meaningful

These drills are slower; the teacher is less concerned with 100% correct responses.

Guessing drills
Whole-class practice: students use yes/no questions to find something out — e.g. guess a classmate's favorite kind of film, a hidden object, where someone is traveling to next, or what a classmate cooked last night.
Using visuals
Teacher shows a picture without revealing it, giving clues only ("It's a photo of a street. There are some cyclists in it."). Use magazines, photos, postcards — creates a genuine information gap.
Imaginary scenarios
"I've just moved to a new house, but I haven't got much money, so I'm gradually buying things for the kitchen." Students ask "Have you bought a kettle yet?" or use question tags.
The Invitation
"My neighbor has invited me to a party, but if she phones me, I'll say I'm ill. What's your excuse?" — students respond with their own first conditional excuse.

Example: a guessing drill

L: Is it a team sport? T: Yes / No.
L: Do you play outside? T: Yes / No.
L: Do you need special equipment? ...and so on.

Your turn: make it meaningful. Each drill below works, but nobody finds out anything about anyone. In pairs, turn each one into a meaningful drill, using one of the four ideas above.

1. T: buy a kettle L: Have you bought a kettle yet? (round the class, one prompt each)
2. T: cook, last night L: What did you cook last night? (repeated after the teacher)
3. T: swim L: I can swim. (each learner, one verb each)

One way each

1. An imaginary scenario. “I’ve just moved into a flat with no money, so I’m buying things slowly.” The class asks “Have you bought a kettle yet?”, and the teacher answers from a list only she can see, until they find the three things she still needs.

2. A guessing drill. One learner thinks of what they really ate last night; the others ask yes or no questions (“Did you cook pasta?”, “Did you eat out?”) until someone gets it.

3. Find someone who. Each learner has six skills on a card (whistle, juggle, ride a horse…) and asks round the class, “Can you whistle?”, to find a name for each.

What makes each one meaningful: there is something to find out, and the answer is real.

A way of drilling sentences to a beat, often set to music, popularised by Carolyn Graham. Easy to create your own: follow the beat. This one is made up for this session.

controlled practice
Transformation and substitution drills Practice

Transformation drill: the teacher gives a cue, learners transform the sentence.

T: I get up early every day. (Mei)
L: Mei gets up early every day.
T: I have a bath.
L: She has a bath.
T: I have breakfast at eight o'clock.
L: She has ... etc.

Substitution drill: the teacher gives a new word, learners substitute it into the sentence.

T: I always play tennis on Saturdays. (sometimes)
L: I sometimes play tennis on Saturdays. ...and so on.

Carlos and Ingrid: a shopping information gap Less-guided practice
Carlos: I'm just going to the market. Do you need anything?
Ingrid: No, I don't think so. Oh, wait. We've run out of rice.
Carlos: Don't worry. It's on my list. I'm going to get some.
Ingrid: What about eggs?
Carlos: OK. I'll go to the farm stall and buy a dozen.

Extension: draw a picture of the scene.

The key

On the page: the nine technique matches, the thirteen drill types with an example each after checking, the exit ticket has none. The cued dialogue has no key; any line that fits the cue is right.

Watch for

  • A drill that never reaches natural speed. Every drill on this page ends at the speed the sentence is said in the street.
  • Drilling without meaning. The film is the warning: perfect form, no idea what it means.
  • Time. Part 3 can take the whole hour; thirteen types in ten minutes means you read the examples, they do not.
