Types of drill Example, then your turn

Let's look at one together first: a chain drill.

Chain drill: practice passes from student to student like a chain. One asks or says something to their neighbour, who responds and then turns to the next student.
T → S1: How are you?
S1 → S2: I'm fine, thanks. How are you?
S2 → S3: I'm fine too. How are you? ...and so on around the room.

More meaningful drills Presentation

These drills are slower; the teacher is less concerned with 100% correct responses.

Guessing drills
Whole-class practice: students use yes/no questions to find something out — e.g. guess a classmate's favourite sport, an illness, where someone is emigrating to, or what a classmate did last night.
Using visuals
Teacher shows a picture without revealing it, giving clues only ("It's a picture of a room. There are some people in it."). Use magazines, photos, postcards — creates a genuine information gap.
Imaginary scenarios
"I've just rented a new flat, but I haven't got much money, so I'm gradually buying furniture." Students ask "Have you bought a bed yet?" or use question tags.
The Meeting
"My boss wants a meeting, but if he rings me, I'll say I'm busy. What's your excuse?" — students respond with their own first conditional excuse.

Example: a guessing drill

L: Is it a team sport? T: Yes / No.
L: Do you play outside? T: Yes / No.
L: Do you need special equipment? ...and so on.

Jazz chants Rhythm

A way of drilling sentences to a beat, often set to music, published by Carolyn Graham (Oxford American English), most famously "Small Talk" and "Jazz Chants". Usually chanted in American English. Easy to create your own: follow the beat.

A: Hi. How are you? B: Fine. How are you?
I'm fine. How's Bill? He's fine.
How's Mary? She's fine.
How are the children? They're fine.
How's your job? It's fine.
How's Jack? He's sick!
Oh no!!!!!

Transformation and substitution drills Practice

Transformation drill: the teacher gives a cue, learners transform the sentence.

T: I get up early every day. (Ayşe)
L: Ayşe gets up early every day.
T: I have a bath.
L: She has a bath.
T: I have breakfast at eight o'clock.
L: She has ... etc.

Substitution drill: the teacher gives a new word, learners substitute it into the sentence.

T: I always play tennis on Saturdays. (sometimes)
L: I sometimes play tennis on Saturdays. ...and so on.

The key

On the page: the nine technique matches, the thirteen drill types with an example each after checking, the exit ticket has none. The cued dialogue has no key; any line that fits the cue is right.

Watch for

  • A drill that never reaches natural speed. Every drill on this page ends at the speed the sentence is said in the street.
  • Drilling without meaning. The film is the warning: perfect form, no idea what it means.
  • Time. Part 3 can take the whole hour; thirteen types in ten minutes means you read the examples, they do not.

Sources. The drilling techniques follow Donn Byrne, Teaching Oral English (Longman, 1986). The jazz chant is Carolyn Graham's (Jazz Chants, Oxford University Press). Video: The Pink Panther (2006, MGM), the dialect-coach scene, on the Movieclips channel.
