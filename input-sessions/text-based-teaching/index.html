What makes a lesson text-based?
A lesson where a reading or listening text supplies both the topic and the source of the target language — rather than a grammar point being chosen first and a text built to fit it.

Staging a text-based lesson

A text-based lesson starts from a text — a reading or a listening — chosen for its content and its language together, not from a grammar point chosen first. It opens with a lead-in that creates interest in the topic, often a prediction task from a title or image. A gist task comes next, testing global understanding only: skim reading under a time limit, or hearing the audio once. Only once gist is secure does a detail task follow, prompting a closer, slower pass. From there the lesson turns to language: target items are lifted straight from the text itself, wherever they occur, rather than pre-selected — sometimes several different items, not just one. Meaning is usually checked with CCQs the teacher asks live, or with a short guided-discovery task on a handout where students work out meaning from context themselves before the teacher confirms it — either way, meaning comes before form. Form is then boarded: the teacher elicits it from students where possible, filling gaps rather than presenting the whole rule from scratch, since some of it is often visible in the text already. Pronunciation gets the same direct attention as in any other framework — the teacher models the item, marks stress and weak forms on the board or a handout, and drills chorally then individually; it is not left implicit just because the item came from a text. The lesson closes with a follow-on task that responds to the text's content — a discussion, a role play, or a piece of writing. The text drives the topic and supplies the language, but clarifying that language — meaning, form and pronunciation — still takes real, direct teaching time; it does not happen automatically just because students met the item while reading.

Your turn

Find it in the text

A short text, and a language point hidden in it. In pairs, write the task that gets learners to find the language themselves. Then write two CCQs for one sentence. Then swap with another pair and say whether each task works.

Mira’s year in Lisbon · B1

Mira moved to Lisbon last September. She has lived in the same flat since then, and she has already made three close friends: her neighbor Tomás, who has played the guitar for twenty years, and two colleagues from the café where she works. She hasn’t found a job in her own field yet, but she has started a Portuguese course, and her teacher says she has made fast progress. “I’ve never felt so far from home,” she says, “or so happy.”

The target language is the present perfect for things that connect the past with now. It is in the text several times. Your learners do not know that: your task is how they find it.

One way to do it

Where it is: has lived … since then; has already made; has played … for twenty years; hasn’t found … yet; has started; has made fast progress; I’ve never felt.

The task: “Read the text again. Find the sentences about things that started in the past and still matter now. Underline the verbs. What do they look like? Work with your partner.” Then pairs compare, and the teacher boards two of them.

CCQs: Did she move to Lisbon in the past? (Yes: last September.) Does she live in the flat now? (Yes.) Is the time finished? (No: it continues.) Can we say “She lived in the same flat since then”? (No.)

Two traps to look for in the swap

Naming it: “Find the present perfect” tells the learners the answer. The task should send them to the meaning, and the form follows.

Too much: “Underline all the verbs” gives them twenty things and no idea which matters. Narrow it to one meaning.
