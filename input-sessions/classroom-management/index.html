Your turn: rescue this teacher Rewrite it
“Right, so what we're gonna do is, er, I'm gonna put you into groups, but before that there are a few things we've gotta sort out. So just jot down, if you've got a pencil, could you write this, then when we've finished that we're going to move on to the next bit, which involves…”

The task: learners read five questions, then ask a partner and write down the answers. Write the instructions in short steps, and add one question to check they've understood.

  1. “Work with your partner.” (gesture: you two, you two)
  2. “Read the five questions. Don't write.” (hold up the handout)
  3. “Ask your partner the questions. Write their answers.”
  4. “Five minutes.” (show five fingers)
Check: “Are you writing your answers, or your partner's?” (My partner's.) Then demonstrate the first question with one student before everyone starts.

    Trainer notes Tutor only

    Before

    Groups of three with one device each for the wheel; the rest is whole class on the screen. Nothing to print.

    Running it

    StageWhat you do
    Would you ratherSix dilemmas, fast. Pick one, tell your partner why, next. The point is that they already have a theory of good teaching; this session gives it names.
    Who is talkingSet the sliders to their TP class: the real number of students, the real minutes. Watch the bar. Then the six cards: ask them to predict the six reasons before clicking.
    The wheelThrees. Each group spins and talks for two minutes per area; they will get through five or six, not thirteen. Collect one line per group at the end.
    Yes, no or maybePairs decide, then reveal one at a time with the whole room. Number 7 (repeating what a student said) is the one to stop on: it is the echo they all do.
    Spot the problemWhole class on screen, one moment at a time, a different trainee each time. These are Scrivener’s fourteen; say so at the end, not the start.
    Grade your languageThe six fixes as a class, then the rescue in pairs. Compare two or three rewrites against the model on screen.
    Into TPEach trainee ticks two, not eight, and says which two.

    The key

    Everything reveals on the page: the twelve yes-no-maybe answers with their reasons, the fourteen problems with their labels, the six graded instructions and the model rescue.

    Watch for

    • The wheel eating the session. Twelve minutes is the limit; stop it with the sentence from the page: "which of these is the hardest for you, and why?"
    • Capital letters on the board: they will argue. The reveal gives the reason (they hide which words take a capital); hold the line.
    • The rescue rewrite coming out as polite questions. Imperatives, and a check question that is not "do you understand?"

    Sources. The moments in "Spot the problem" and the statements in yes, no or maybe are made up for this session. The ideas behind them draw on Jim Scrivener, Learning Teaching (Macmillan), the chapter on classroom management.
