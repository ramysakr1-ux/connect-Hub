PART 1

Although flipped learning is a specific framework or approach, there are many ways to implement it in the learning process. However, in a nutshell, flipped learning is a reversal in the standard model of teaching by “delivering instruction to students at home through self-study materials and moving the ‘homework’ element to the classroom” (Harrison 2013). This means the lesson input (the stage which involves an explanation, a lecture or reading or listening) is moved from inside the classroom to outside the classroom.

PART 2

The origins and history of flipped learning are a remarkably uncomplicated story. Two US high school teachers, Jonathan Bergmann and Aaron Sams, were irritated by the necessity to repeat lessons for students who had been absent. They came across some screen-capturing software, so they started recording their lessons. They made their lessons available online for their learners to watch in their own time. What Bergmann and Sams soon discovered was that their learners were able to access their recorded lessons at a time which suited them, be it at home on their laptops or during free periods at school on their phones and tablets.

PART 3

Given that the key principle of flipped learning is that the learners do the input part of the lesson at home, it is therefore imperative that they actually do this. This means that there is an element of transferring responsibility to the learners. This could be an area of concern for a teacher, especially if the teacher feels some of their learners are not responsible or autonomous enough.

However, there is a solution to this problem. If a learner comes to class and they haven’t watched the video, done the reading or research, they can then do this in class. That way, their class time is not wasted, they aren’t a step behind, they still get the input, and soon they will see that by not doing the work outside of class, they are not keeping up with their peers.

Alternatively, you can put the learner with one who has done the preparation and get them to explain the language or what was in the reading text. While this is happening, you can ask all the learners in the class to check with their partners what they had done outside of class as a quick warmer or refresher, and that way everyone is on the same page.

At the end of the day, preparing for class is the learner’s responsibility. The teacher can only provide them with the tools and the opportunity; it is the learner who has to actually take the decision to prepare. If enough classes follow the flipped format, then it will soon become apparent to learners who aren’t preparing for class that they aren’t getting the most out of lessons, and they will start to do the work outside of class, too.

PART 4

A basic principle of flipped learning is to take advantage of technology and let learners use their own time and technology for lesson input. This means class time can be used more effectively for encouraging and reinforcing learning. In a flipped classroom, class time is often used:

  • to do further exercises or controlled practice
  • to revise main ideas and key points
  • to work on a project in groups or as a whole class

This list is by no means exhaustive, and the main idea is that the learners are putting what they have learned outside of class into practice inside class, with the teacher alongside to support.

PART 5

Even though flipped learning was born out of general education, it is still applicable to ELT. Whereas in mainstream education a teacher will lecture their learners on a given topic, an English language teacher will provide learners with target language presented in a clear context, such as a text. So, it is this text which can be taken outside of the classroom, to be read at home and prepared in advance of class. Alternatively, a teacher in ELT might want to go down the path of creating videos with language explanations and examples, just like what Bergmann and Sams originally did.

Either way, there is a question around where best to store these texts and videos and how best to make them accessible to learners. Some schools might have their own learning management system where they could be stored, but if this is not an option you could consider a video site, a shared drive or a file-sharing folder.

PART 6

Take a double-page spread of a coursebook. You will probably find a reading or a listening text or a grammar point. Give this to the learners to do at home. If it is a text, ask them to read it and work through the vocabulary at home. If it is a grammar point, ask them to go through it and do some of the exercises at home.

Use class time to follow up and reinforce what they have done at home. If we consider the work done outside of class the Present stage of a PPP lesson framework, then class time is the Practice and Produce stages. When it comes to skills work, the more detailed stages, such as bottom-up processing when listening or editing and redrafting a text, could all be done during class time. The learners prepare and do the initial reading, listening or writing at home, but the heavier work, the meatier part, is done in class. This might also help to avoid those skills lessons where you simply run out of time to finish the reading or the writing, as a significant proportion of the material would have been prepared in advance of class.

You could use the class time for those extended follow-up activities that you usually never have enough time for, such as project work, writing a response to what has been read, or producing a news report. The time could also be dedicated to further practice as well as looking at related topics and any questions or issues the learners have.

PART 7

All in all, flipping your classroom should give you the opportunities to do more meaningful and creative activities inside the classroom, where you can give learners the opportunity to practice and develop their linguistic abilities, while leaving the more nitty-gritty part of language learning to be done at home, making use of modern-day technology and encouraging autonomous learning.

From “Flipping the lesson in ELT”, the IH Journal blog (ihworld.com), lightly shortened. It cites Harrison, L. (2013), “The flipped classroom in ELT”, eltjam.com, and flippedclass.com.

Flip a page A coursebook spread

A double-page spread from a pre-intermediate coursebook, unit 4, Work. Decide what goes home, in what order, and what stays in class. Then say what the ninety minutes in class are for.

Page 38

1 Vocabulary. Twelve jobs and workplaces, matched to pictures. A short exercise: a nurse works in a …

2 Reading. Three people, three jobs: a 350-word text, three short profiles. A gist task (match each person to a photo), then six detail questions.

3 Grammar box. Present perfect for experience: I’ve worked in two countries. Have you ever had a night job? A rule to complete, then eight gap-fill sentences.

Page 39

4 Pronunciation. The contracted ’ve and ’s; a listen-and-repeat.

5 Speaking. Find someone who has …: a class mingle with eight prompts, then report back.

6 Writing. A profile of a person you know and their work, 100 words, using the present perfect at least twice.

At home, before the lesson

In class

One way

Home. 1, 2 and 3, in that order: the words first, so the text can be read; the text with its gist and detail tasks, which they check against a key you give them; then the grammar box and the rule, and the first four gap-fills. That is the Present stage, and the reading.

Class. Two minutes in pairs to compare the text answers and the rule (your check on who did it). A quick drill of the contracted forms (4), which needs you. Then the last four gap-fills as a race, the mingle (5) at full length, the report back, and the writing (6) started in class with you walking round, finished at home. Nothing was presented; everything was practiced.

What changed. The text and the grammar would have taken half the lesson. The speaking, which is the part that needs the room and the teacher, gets the time it never gets.

A reading TP, flipped

Before. The text with its gist question and the six detail questions, and the eight pre-taught words with their meanings, on one sheet or one link. “Read it, answer the questions, check them against the key on the second page, and bring one question of your own.”

Where. The course’s shared folder, and a paper copy for the two students who never open the folder.

The first five minutes. Pairs compare their detail answers and their own questions. Anyone who did not read it reads the first profile now while the others compare, and joins at the question stage.

The room. The students’ own questions on the board, answered by the class. Then the part a reading TP never reaches: a response task, a discussion, a role-play from the text, with you monitoring and a language feedback stage at the end on what you heard.

What was lost: the lead-in and the prediction stage, which depend on the text being new. Not every lesson wants flipping. A text whose whole point is the first reading in the room should keep it.
