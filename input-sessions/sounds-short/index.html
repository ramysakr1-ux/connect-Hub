What you're actually learning to teach

Hearing "ship" and "sheep" apart is the easy part: you already can. The job is teaching a student who genuinely can't yet: knowing why they can't, what to do with your mouth to show the difference, and which pairs are worth drilling for their L1. That's what the next two sections are for.

Lead-in 2 minutes

Listen: "ship" then "sheep." Same spelling pattern almost, one letter of difference on the page. Can you hear which is which without seeing the words?

Where the sound is made

Click each sound to hear it, then say it back yourself and notice where your own tongue, lips or teeth move.

Consonants are formed when airflow from the lungs is obstructed by the moveable parts of the mouth: tongue, teeth, lips, palate. Click a sound to hear it in a word and see where it's obstructed.

Articulation: how to physically produce it

A learner can't copy a written symbol: they need to see the mouth move. On the site below, click each of these four sounds on the real IPA chart and watch the video, then write down what you actually see (tongue, lips, teeth) before checking it against the summary underneath.

Show the real thing rather than a diagram: Seeing Speech is a clickable IPA chart with real ultrasound, MRI and animated vocal-tract video for every sound, free.seeingspeech.ac.uk

Now try it yourself: clap for what you heard 15 minutes

The same drill, from the student's seat. Hit Play to hear the word (Play again repeats it), then clap the pad you heard. The next Play gives the next word, the same one for everyone in the room: the technique you'd run live with a class.

Dictation: sound to spelling 13 minutes

English spelling doesn't reliably show pronunciation. Listen and pick the word that matches the sound, not the sound that matches the usual spelling.
