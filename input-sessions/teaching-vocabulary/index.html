Which part is missing?

Each student "knows" the word. Which part of knowing it is still missing?

Two kinds of knowingRecognizing a word when you meet it is not the same as using it. Most learners understand far more words than they produce, and a word you teach for reading needs less time than one you want them to say and write.

Which words, and how many

A B1 reading lesson. The students read for the gist, then answer the detail questions, then discuss. Read the text and the tasks, then decide what you would do with each word in bold.

Mina opened her café on a quiet side street in 2019. For the first year, hardly anyone came in, and she was close to giving up. Then a food blogger wrote about her cinnamon buns, and the next morning there was a line around the block.

Today she employs six people and bakes four hundred buns a day. "The secret is the dough," she says. "I leave it overnight." Her landlord has offered her the shop next door, but she isn't sure. "Bigger isn't always better. I don't want to lose what makes this place cozy."

The tasks

  1. Gist: why did the café suddenly get busy?
  2. Detail: was the first year a good year? Who has offered Mina more space?
  3. Discussion: should Mina take the shop next door? Describe a cozy place you know.
How to decideTeach before the text only what the tasks depend on, and favor words that are useful: frequent, wide in use, and worth keeping. A word the text explains, the students can guess. A word nobody needs, you gloss if someone asks and move on. Coursebooks rarely present more than about a dozen new words at once, and fewer when the students are to use them, not only understand them.

Translation: true or false?

Try it: what does reckon mean?

Your tutor reads five sentences, with a pause after each. Don't say anything until all five are done. Then tell your partner what you think it means.

  1. I reckon it's going to rain later. Take an umbrella.
  2. How much do you reckon an apartment like this costs?
  3. She'll be here soon, I reckon.
  4. Do you reckon he's telling the truth?
  5. "Is the film any good?" "Yes, I reckon you'll like it."
What just happenedReckon means think or guess, an opinion, and it is informal. You heard it five times, met its grammar (reckon + a clause, do you reckon), and worked the meaning out yourself. That is slower than a translation, and that is why it stays.
Meaning firstShow the meaning, then give the word: the meaning makes them want the form. Keep the two close together. The longer the gap between the meaning and the word, the less likely the students are to connect them.

Saying it

Model the word naturally two or three times before anyone repeats it. Give them a few seconds to say it to themselves first, a mumble drill, then drill it chorally and individually. Show the stress with your fingers or a mark on the board.

Writing it

Don't keep the spelling back for long. Students form a picture of the spelling as soon as they hear a word, so give them the right one. Keep one part of the board for new words only, and leave time in the lesson for them to copy it.

Putting words to work

Repeating a word is not enough. Words stay when students make decisions about them, and some decisions take more thinking than others. Five kinds of task, from least to most demanding: identify, select, match, sort, rank. Which kind is each activity?

Do one: the odd one out

In pairs. Which is the odd one out in each group, and why? Write your reasons.

  1. sofa  ·  wardrobe  ·  fridge  ·  bookcase
  2. angry  ·  nervous  ·  furious  ·  cross
  3. commute  ·  deadline  ·  salary  ·  weekend
There is no right answerThe fridge needs electricity, or the sofa is the one you sit on, or the bookcase is the only one with "book" in it: any answer with a reason counts. In 2, nervous is not anger; in 3, weekend is the only one that is not about work, or deadline the only one with a fixed date. The thinking is the point. Every reason you gave used the words again.

Do one: rank it

You are camping for three nights and can carry five of these. Choose and order them, most important first, then agree a list with your partner.

a tent  ·  a flashlight  ·  a frying pan  ·  a pocket knife  ·  matches  ·  a sleeping bag  ·  a first-aid kit  ·  a map  ·  a phone charger  ·  insect repellent

What the task didYou read every word, decided what each one is for, compared them, defended your order, and gave way on some. That is more work on ten words than any drill, and the words are used in sentences you made yourselves. In class, the students need the words first: present, then let them rank.

Plan the review

You are presenting eight words for clothes: shirt, jacket, jeans, dress, sweater, socks, scarf, boots. Write the order you would go in, with the moments you go back to earlier words.

One way to do itpresent shirt · present jacket · present jeans · back to shirt · back to jeans · present dress · back to jacket · present sweater · back to dress · back to shirt · present socks · and so on, a little further apart each time, in a different order. Then the next lesson starts with them again, and the lesson after that.

Word cards

The word on one side, the meaning or a translation on the other. Look at the word and recall the meaning, then turn the pile round and recall the word. Shuffle, so the order doesn't do the remembering. The hard ones go to the top; the learned ones come out.

The notebook

Your board is the model for their notebooks. Every so often, ask students to compare notebooks: is the stress marked, is the part of speech there, is a chunk written as a chunk? Begin the next lesson with a quiet review.
