To begin

So — where next?

Four weeks ago most of you had never planned a lesson. Talk to the person next to you for three minutes:

  • Which country, and why that one?
  • Which age group would you actually choose, now that you've taught some?
  • What's the one thing about teaching you most want to get better at?
  • Where do you want to be in a year — same job, different country, further qualification?
Worth saying out loud A CELTA gets you into the profession; it doesn't finish the training. Almost everything that makes someone good at this job is learned in the two or three years after the course — which is what the second half of this session is about.
First

Your CV, covering letter and referees

You'll need a CV and a covering letter. You'll also need referees — your tutors are normally happy to act as one, and your CELTA report is itself a reference, so send it with your application.

What a post-CELTA CV should make obvious in ten seconds

  • The qualification, in full: Cambridge CELTA, the centre, the date. Put it near the top, not at the bottom under hobbies.
  • What you actually taught: the levels, the number of assessed hours, the age group, whether it was in-person or online. "Six assessed hours across A1–B2 adult groups" says far more than "completed teaching practice".
  • Coursebooks you've handled. Make a note of them before you forget — you will be asked.
  • Anything else that transfers: other languages you speak, previous work with children or in training, admin or tech skills.
  • One page if you can, two at most. Clean, no photo unless the country expects it.

The covering letter

  • Name the school and say why that school — one sentence of evidence that you've read their website.
  • Say what you can teach and what you'd like to learn. Schools like new teachers who want feedback.
  • Keep it to three short paragraphs. Attach the CELTA report.
Do this while it's fresh Write the CV this week, before the details fade — your levels, your books, your assessed hours, your tutors' names and emails. Ask your tutors now whether they're happy to be named, rather than in four months' time.

Closer to home

Finding work in Istanbul

How people actually get hired here

  • Turn up. Visit schools in person and leave your CV. It still works here in a way it doesn't in most places.
  • Get on the job lists. Ask your tutors which mailing lists and teacher groups are worth joining.
  • Facebook groups. There are long-running Istanbul teaching groups where jobs are posted, and separate "blacklist" and "greenlist" groups where teachers name schools that pay late — or pay properly. Read those before you accept an offer.
  • September and January are the two big hiring moments. Out of season, cover work is how you get a foot in.

The hard part

The interview

Expect to be asked for a demo lesson, and sometimes a lesson plan to go with it. It is normal — don't be caught out by it. Find out as much as you can about the school beforehand, starting with their website.

If they ask for a demo Ask three questions first: what level, how long, and how many students. Then plan something you can actually deliver — a clear context, a short clarification stage, and as much student talking time as you can build in. They are watching whether the students talk, not whether your materials are beautiful.

Questions to ask them

Tick the ones that matter to you and take the list with you.

Questions they'll ask you

  1. Why did you decide to be a teacher, and why this country?
  2. How did you find the CELTA, and what did you learn?
  3. What are your strengths as a teacher, and what would you like to develop?
  4. What levels do you prefer teaching? What have you taught?
  5. What books have you used?
  6. How would you motivate a group of disinterested students?
  7. How would you get students to speak in class?
  8. How would you teach [a structure or a set of vocabulary]?
  9. Why do you want to work here?
  10. What are your professional development plans?

"What would you like to develop?"

Never say "nothing". Name one real thing from your TPs — timing, instructions, error correction — and say what you're doing about it. It reads as self-awareness, not weakness.

"What are your professional development plans?"

This is the question this session exists for. Have an answer: an association you're joining, a qualification you're aiming at, something you're reading. Two sentences is plenty.

"How would you teach X?"

Talk them through a shape, not a trick: how you'd set the context, how you'd check meaning, how you'd get them saying it, then practice. You have just spent a month doing exactly this.

"What levels have you taught?"

Be precise and honest. Two levels taught properly is a better answer than a vague claim to have taught everything.

The paperwork

Working legally, and settling in

Residence and work permits

  • Residence permit applications go through the Turkish migration service — start at e-ikamet.goc.gov.tr, with general information at goc.gov.tr.
  • A work permit is applied for by the employer, not by you. Outside state institutions and the larger schools it is less common than it should be — and without one you are not entitled to state healthcare, so you'll need residency and your own insurance.
  • Ask about this at interview, in plain terms, and get the answer in writing.

The point of all this

Carrying on developing

Three kinds of development: things you do on your own, things you do with other people, and things you get a certificate for. You need all three, and the first is free.

On your own, starting Monday

  • Keep a teaching journal. Five lines after each lesson: what worked, what didn't, what you'd change. After a term you'll have something no observation can give you.
  • Record yourself. Audio is enough. Listen for how much you talk and how long your instructions are. It is uncomfortable and it works faster than anything else.
  • Read one thing a week. An article, a blog post, a chapter. Not a book a month — that never happens.
  • Keep your CELTA folder. The frameworks you were given are still the frameworks. Go back to them when a lesson goes wrong.

With other people

  • Peer observation. Ask a colleague if you can watch them, and offer the same back. Most teachers say yes and almost nobody asks.
  • Staff-room workshops. Schools that run them are the schools worth working for. Volunteer to give one in your first year — it's the fastest way to be taken seriously.
  • Teacher associations. Cheap, and they run the conferences and the webinars.
